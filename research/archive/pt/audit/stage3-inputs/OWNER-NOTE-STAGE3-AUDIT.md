### 1. An exotic cone has a single, decisive witness pair

Suppose K satisfies all three hypotheses. Because both cones are self-dual, neither contains the other. Therefore,
there is some X ∈ K \ Q_3. Since X is not positive semidefinite, it has a negative eigenvalue. Choose a
corresponding normalized eigenvector ψ, and define P_ψ = |ψ⟩⟨ψ|. Then Tr(X P_ψ) < 0. Self-duality immediately
implies P_ψ ∉ K, because every two elements of a self-dual cone must have nonnegative inner product. Consequently,
one exact pair (X, P_ψ) witnesses both failures simultaneously. This gives Thread X a useful certificate format.
The previously identified (E0, G) and (F, T_ψ) pairs illustrate the obstruction, although a candidate countermodel
must still establish that its proposed X actually belongs to K.

### 2. The ten-dimensional reduction has a direct proof

Write U = cnot. Since CNOT is an orthogonal involution, H2 implies U(K) = K. Define the orthogonal projection onto
its fixed subspace: P_+ = (I + U)/2. Convexity and invariance give P_+(K) = K ∩ V_+. For any v ∈ V_+, its pairing
with an arbitrary k ∈ K equals its pairing with P_+ k. Therefore (K ∩ V_+)^{*_{V_+}} = K ∩ V_+. So the
ten-dimensional fixed-subspace cone really must be self-dual, using the inherited inner product. One limitation
remains: uniqueness of the ten-dimensional intersection would not, by itself, prove uniqueness of the full
sixteen-dimensional cone. The lifting step is a distinct obligation.

### 3. Every candidate lies inside a precise admissibility corridor

Let S be the cone generated by all product states and define C = cone(S ∪ U(S)). Then H1 and H2 imply C ⊆ K, while
H3 gives C ⊆ K ⊆ C*. This establishes exact inner and outer bounds for both research threads. There is also a useful
normalization result: every nonzero X ∈ K has strictly positive trace. Product-state positivity gives nonnegative
expectation values on all product pure states. Their average is I/4; if it vanished, every such expectation would
vanish, forcing X = 0, because product projectors span the Hermitian space. Thus the trace-one section of K is
compact.
