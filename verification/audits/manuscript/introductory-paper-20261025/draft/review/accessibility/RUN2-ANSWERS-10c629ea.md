1. "Observational incompleteness" is the idea that every observer is part of a larger, finite world that runs by fixed, reversible rules, and that the observer can only ever register part of that world's state. The rest is hidden. The paper stresses that hidden means out of reach in principle, not just not yet measured. A player in a card game who sees only the face-up cards is in this position, and so is any observer inside a closed system who can only read some of its variables, the "visible" ones.

2. The whole world moves by a rule that sends each complete state to exactly one next state, so nothing is lost and nothing is random. But you only see part of the state. Two complete states can look the same to you and still differ in their hidden parts, and those two can lead to different things you see next. From your side, the same visible situation is followed sometimes by one result and sometimes by another, and that looks like chance. The paper's card example: one card face up, one face down, and each shuffle swaps them. After one shuffle you can't predict the colour showing any better than a coin toss, because it depends on the card you can't see. After a second shuffle the original card is back for certain. The uncertainty is the player's own ignorance, not something in the rules.

3. Over any fixed, finite number of steps, three collections of possible observation records turn out to be identical:
   - every probability pattern you could write down;
   - every pattern a finite, reversible, deterministic world with a hidden part can show an observer;
   - every pattern you get from a finite quantum system measured the same way at every step.

   So any random-looking record, including any record of repeated fixed quantum measurements, could have come from a partly hidden deterministic world, and the reverse also holds. One simple construction stores a whole pre-drawn sequence plus a clock in the hidden part and reveals one entry per step. "On a finite time horizon" matters because a finite deterministic world must eventually return to its starting state, so its patterns repeat. The match is exact only over a chosen stretch of time. A pattern that never repeats can't be reproduced forever by one fixed finite world.

4. The result shows only that such a hidden-world explanation is possible. It doesn't show that nature works this way. It covers too much and too little at once:
   - It works for every pattern, so it picks out nothing specific about quantum theory. It doesn't explain why probabilities are squared amplitudes; the squaring is put in by hand. It also doesn't fix which evolution links two times.
   - It covers only one fixed measurement repeated each step. It doesn't cover incompatible measurements, superpositions, or combining systems.
   - It gives a representation of the statistics, not a physical mechanism.

   To get full quantum mechanics, the paper says five extra "completion conditions" on what operations are available must hold. Roughly: probabilities behave sensibly; adding a trivial helper system or an untouched bystander changes nothing; every reversible quantum operation on a combined system is available; and you can bring in a fresh, evenly mixed helper, use it and throw it away. These conditions are shown to be independent and not implied by observational incompleteness. Whether a real physical substrate supplies them is left as the main open question. The paper also shows that no single finite hidden world can reproduce everything a qubit can do across all experiments, and that Bell correlations need one lab's hidden response to depend on the other lab's setting.

5. "Memory" means the next observation depends on earlier observations, not just the current one. Whenever the record has memory, every hidden world that reproduces it must store that memory in the hidden part, and the hidden part must be big enough to hold it. Conversely, if any one such world keeps a trace of the past that changes what is seen next, the record has memory. The paper separates keeping the past from memory: a reversible world stores its whole past anyway, and memory is only the part of that store that affects what you see next. From the visible side, the observer can:
   - put a lower bound on how many hidden states there must be (roughly 2 to the power of the largest measured memory in bits);
   - pin down the minimum amount of hidden predictive memory any deterministic completion must carry;
   - get a floor on memory from how differently two pasts predict the next step.

   There is also a result about "chaining" forecasts step by step. If the hidden part starts with the same odds whatever is visible, then any visible step that isn't fully predictable means that, somewhere in the world's cycle, forecasts can't be built up step by step. With every starting point having some probability, this is equivalent to there being memory at some order before the cycle returns. That memory might lie beyond any window an observer can actually access.

6. Cosmological constant: summing the "jitter" energy of empty space gives a number about 120 orders of magnitude too large. In this view, that zero-point sum belongs to the observer's emergent quantum description. Gravity's source would instead be defined at the level of the underlying world. What the paper establishes is that adding a constant to all quantum energies changes no visible probability, so the quantum description can't tell whether that offset gravitates. It then proposes field equations under which the zero-point sum wouldn't curve spacetime, so the mismatch would need no cancellation. What it leaves open is deriving those gravitational field equations from the underlying world. It also doesn't compute the observed value of the constant; that value is taken as an input.

   Black holes: the paper separates two questions. One is whether information is conserved, which is automatic here because the dynamics is reversible. The other is whether someone reading only the outgoing radiation can recover it, and the answer is not necessarily. Small exact computer checks show the two come apart. In some reversible toy worlds a message stays completely hidden from someone reading the emitted registers. That is possible only when the unread registers have at least as many joint values as there are messages. And some irreversible worlds can still reveal a message exactly. What is left open is whether the real situation follows the usual "Page curve" of entanglement. That rests on conditions the paper says are not established, and one of them, a thorough-scrambling condition, actually fails for the simple type of dynamics considered. Also open are the field equations, the case where the black hole loses energy as it radiates, and the link to the newer "island" calculations.

7. The hardest part was section 2.5 on "P-divisibility" and "rooted transition matrices." I followed the weather-forecast intuition, but I'm not confident I understood the conditions: why "a hidden prior shared by every root and drawn independently of it" matters, and why rows for starting states that never occur must be included. I mostly guessed at those. Section 3.1, which separates memory, failure of chaining and "quantum non-Markovianity," was also dense. I took its message to be that these are three different things, but the Hadamard and Pauli-twirl examples assumed background I don't have. The five completion conditions read as a list, and I couldn't tell which ones a real physical world would plausibly fail. In the cosmological-constant section, the "proposed resolution" felt more like a restatement than a solution, since the key field equations aren't derived; to its credit, the paper says so. The Bell section's terms ("ontic parameter dependence," "H-Bell," "coupling graph") pointed to other papers and weren't fully explained here. Finally, the abstract is very compressed and hard to read before the body; I understood it only after finishing the paper.
