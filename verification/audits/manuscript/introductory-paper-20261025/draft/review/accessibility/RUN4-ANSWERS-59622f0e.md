1. "Observational incompleteness" is the idea that any real observer is itself part of the world it watches. That world is finite, deterministic and reversible, and the observer can only ever see some of its variables. The rest is not just unknown so far. It is out of reach in principle. The paper's picture is a card game. Two cards lie on a table, one face up and one face down, and a fixed shuffle swaps them each step. The player sees only the face-up card. Nothing random happens, but the player is uncertain because one card is always hidden. The paper also stresses that this is not the usual quantum "you can't measure both" kind of limit. It applies even when you only ever look at one fixed thing.

2. When the hidden part of a world differs, the same visible state can lead to different futures. You see the same thing now, but what comes next depends on what you can't see, so the next step looks random to you. In the two-card game, after one swap the visible card is equally likely to be either colour, because you don't know what the face-down card was. After a second swap the original card is back, with certainty. My own example: a dealer works through a deck that was secretly put in a fixed order. Every draw follows from that order, but you don't know the order, so each draw looks like chance. The randomness is in your ignorance, not in the rules. That is also how the paper proves its main result: the hidden part stores a pre-drawn sequence plus a clock, and each step reveals the next entry.

3. Pick any fixed number of time steps. The paper shows that three sets of possible records are the same set:
   - every possible pattern of random-looking observations;
   - every pattern a deterministic, reversible world with a hidden part can show to someone who sees only part of it;
   - every pattern you can get by measuring a finite quantum system the same fixed way at every step.

   So over a limited stretch of time, a record of one repeated quantum measurement has no statistical fingerprint that a hidden deterministic world couldn't copy, and the reverse is also true. "On a finite time horizon" matters because a finite reversible world eventually cycles back to where it started, so its records are periodic in the long run. One world is built to match a pattern over a chosen number of steps, not forever. A pattern that never repeats can't be reproduced exactly for all time by one fixed finite world.

4. The result shows that such a world is possible, not that nature is one. Because every pattern whatsoever can be matched, matching quantum records says nothing special. Several things are still missing. The result covers only one fixed measurement repeated over time, not incompatible measurements, superpositions or combined systems. It doesn't pick out the Born rule's squared form, since the construction works the same for any power. It doesn't fix which evolution links two moments. It doesn't explain a physical mechanism. To get full quantum mechanics you'd need the hidden world to meet five extra conditions on what operations are available, and the paper shows the basic hypothesis doesn't imply them. A model with qubits that never creates superpositions is the example. Three more gaps:
   - no single fixed finite world can reproduce everything one qubit can do across all experiments;
   - getting Bell-violating correlations would need one lab's hidden response to depend on the distant lab's setting (an open hypothesis);
   - the Born exponent and the gravity equations are listed as open.

5. A record "has memory" if what comes next depends on more than what you see right now. Two different pasts can end in the same present but lead to different odds. Whenever that happens, every hidden deterministic world that reproduces the record must store a trace of the past in its hidden part, a trace that actually affects what you see next. The converse also holds: if any such world keeps such a trace, the record has memory. The visible memory can never exceed what the hidden part can store. So from what you can see, you can work out lower bounds: a minimum size for the hidden part, and a minimum amount of hidden memory. If two pasts give clearly different predictions, the amount of memory has a measurable floor. The paper also links memory to whether forecasts can be "chained" step by step. Suppose the hidden part starts the same way whatever you see at the start. Then any step that isn't fully predictable makes chaining fail somewhere in the world's cycle. But that failure, and some of the memory, can sit beyond any time window an observer can actually reach. Memory and chaining failure are also not the same thing, and the paper gives examples of each without the other.

6. Cosmological constant: summing quantum vacuum "jitter" gives an energy about 120 orders of magnitude too large. In this framework the quantum description belongs to the observer's partial view, while gravity's source would belong to the underlying world. These sit at different levels. What the paper establishes is that shifting every energy by a constant changes no observable probability. So the vacuum offset doesn't show up anywhere in the quantum statistics, and you can define a gravitational source separately from it. What's left open is the decisive step: deriving gravity's field equations to show that the vacuum offset doesn't curve spacetime. Two versions of the field equations, one where the offset gravitates and one where it doesn't, fit everything visible equally well. Also, the observed value of the constant is put in by hand, not calculated.

   Black holes: the paper splits "is the information conserved?" from "can an observer recover it from what they can read?" Reversibility guarantees conservation, like a burned letter whose words are still encoded in the smoke. Small exhaustive toy models show the two come apart. Some reversible maps hide the message completely from someone reading only the radiation; that requires the unread part to have at least as many possible values as there are messages. Some maps that lose information still let you recover the message. What's left open: the Page-curve result rests on assumptions about scrambling, isolation and constant energy. The paper says the scrambling one fails for the simple reversible maps it has in mind, and the isolation one isn't established. Constant energy doesn't hold for a black hole whose radiation carries energy away. Also open are the full field equations and links to other approaches (islands, quantum extremal surfaces).

7. The hardest part was section 2.5 on recurrence and indivisibility. I had to read the "rooted family" idea several times. That idea keeps forecast rows for starting states that never actually happen. I'm still not sure why a failure that may show up only in forecasts no observer will ever see, or only beyond any reachable window, should count as physically meaningful rather than a feature of the bookkeeping. The red-start/black-start card discussion helped a little but stayed confusing. The difference between "memory," "failure of chaining" and "quantum non-Markovianity" (3.1) was also dense; I followed the claim that they differ but not every example, such as the Hadamard and Pauli-twirl cases. The five completion conditions in 3.2 were stated so abstractly that I couldn't tell what physical demand each one makes, especially (v). I didn't follow the Bell section's "coupling graph" and the H-Bell hypothesis beyond the gist. Least convincing for me: the main theorem is impressive, but its strength comes from matching everything, which the paper itself admits makes it say little about physics. The applications read more like reframings with the decisive step (the field equations) still missing than like results. The heavy use of references to companion papers I couldn't read ([Main], [GR]) also meant I had to take many supporting steps on trust.
