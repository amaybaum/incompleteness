1. "Observational incompleteness" is the idea that every observer is itself a part of a bigger, finite world. That world runs by fixed rules that are reversible and lose no information, but the observer only ever registers part of its state: the "visible" part. The rest, the "hidden" part, isn't just unknown for now. It is in principle out of the observer's reach. The paper also says this is not the same as quantum unsharpness, because it applies even when you always measure the same single thing. Example: you're playing a card game where some cards stay face down. The game follows strict rules, but because you can't see the face-down cards, you can't fully predict what will happen.

2. If the whole world follows a reversible rule, two worlds that look identical to you can differ in their hidden parts. Those hidden differences send them to different visible futures. So, from where you stand, the same visible situation is followed by different outcomes, and it looks random. The paper's example has two cards on a table, one face up and one face down, and every shuffle swaps them. If the face-down card was dealt at random, then after one shuffle you can't predict the visible colour better than a coin toss. After two shuffles the original card is back for certain. Nothing random happens after the deal. The uncertainty comes only from not seeing the hidden card. The same example shows "memory": what you see after two steps depends on what you saw at the start, not just on what is showing now, because the hidden card holds that information.

3. Fix a number of time steps. Then three collections of observation patterns are exactly the same:
   - every possible probability pattern for sequences of observations (random-looking, "stochastic" ones);
   - every pattern a partly hidden, deterministic, reversible finite world can show its observer;
   - every pattern of results from a finite quantum system that is measured the same way at every step, with Born-rule probabilities.

   So any random-looking pattern can be faked by a hidden deterministic world, and by a quantum system too. A repeated fixed measurement in quantum mechanics therefore produces nothing a hidden deterministic world couldn't reproduce over that stretch. One simple construction puts a whole pre-drawn sequence in the hidden register, together with a clock, and reveals one entry per step.

   "On a finite time horizon" means the match holds only up to some chosen number of steps. A finite reversible world eventually repeats itself, so its patterns are periodic in the long run. A pattern that never repeats can't be reproduced forever by one fixed finite world. You get a separate world for each horizon, not one world for all time.

4. The result is a statement about what is possible, not about what is actually the case. It holds for every pattern, so the existence of a hidden deterministic model tells you nothing special about quantum mechanics or about nature. It also covers only one kind of quantum experiment: the same fixed measurement repeated. Real quantum mechanics also has incompatible measurements, superpositions and composite systems. And the result doesn't pick out the squared-amplitude form of the Born rule. That rule is put into the definition of the quantum class, and the permutation constructions would work just as well with any power. Nor does it pick out which quantum evolution connects two times, and it gives no physical mechanism.

   To get full quantum mechanics, the paper says, five "completion conditions" must hold for the operations an experimenter can perform. Three of them are the substantive ones: an untouched bystander system changes nothing, every reversible quantum operation is available, and fresh helper systems can be added and discarded. The paper shows the basic hypothesis does not imply these. It even gives a qubit theory that fits the observation process but is not quantum. What remains is to show that a physical hidden world actually supplies them, along with a principle that selects the Born exponent and the evolution between times. There are also separate obstructions. No single fixed finite hidden world reproduces everything one qubit can do. And Bell correlations need one lab's hidden response to depend on the other lab's setting. Whether the underlying lattice can supply that without any usable signal is an open hypothesis.

5. Memory here means that the next observation depends on the past beyond what the current observation tells you. If the record you see has memory, then that memory must be stored in the hidden part, in every hidden world that reproduces the record, and the hidden part must be big enough to hold it. The converse also holds: if any one reproducing world keeps such a trace that affects what is seen next, the record has memory.

   The paper separates "keeping the past" from memory. A reversible world always stores its whole past somewhere, even when the record is memoryless. Memory is the part of that store that actually changes future observations.

   From the visible data, the observer can bound the hidden side. Measured memory sets a lower bound on how many hidden states there must be, at least 2 to the power of the maximum past–future information. It also fixes the minimum amount of hidden "predictive memory" a deterministic completion must carry. And if two different pasts lead to clearly different next-step odds with decent probability, a simple inequality gives a lower bound on the memory. So something you can't access gets bounded by something you can measure. In a particular coupling model, observed memory also means the hidden part hasn't relaxed to its reference odds, which bounds a mixing time.

5a. The comparison is meant to show what the world's rule does from different visible starting points. That only makes sense if the only thing that differs between those starts is the visible start itself. If the hidden part were prepared differently depending on the start, then two different forecasts might just reflect two different preparations rather than anything about the dynamics. In the card example, if the dealer put the opposite colour face down depending on which card was face up, the "forecast from red" and "forecast from black" would describe two different games, not one game started two ways. The argument also needs every forecast to come from one common preparation, so that forecasts can be meaningfully compared and found to "overlap". With start-dependent setups that link breaks, and you could get or lose the conclusion artificially.

5b. The result is meant to be about the world's rule: what the same dynamics and the same hidden preparation would do from any start the world could have. It is not about which starts happen to show up in a given sample. A start that never occurs is still possible; the dealer just never uses it. Keeping those rows means the table is a property of the physical setup, not of the sample. The paper's second deck example shows why this matters. The observer always sees red with certainty, yet the forecast from the unused black start overlaps with the one from red at step 1. That overlap is what makes step-by-step chaining fail. The price, which the paper admits, is that the failure may then sit only in forecasts nobody will ever see. If every start actually occurs with some probability, this worry goes away.

5c. The mathematics is about the world's full recurrence cycle: the whole stretch until the entire system returns to its starting configuration. The cycle-level statement is this. If the hidden part starts with the same odds whatever is visible, and some visible step is not fully predictable, then somewhere in that cycle the forecasts from the start can't be built up step by step. With every start having some probability, that is equivalent to the record having memory at some time lag shorter than the cycle.

   But the cycle can be enormously long compared with the windows of time an observer can actually watch. A hidden trace of the past might first affect what is seen only at a lag beyond every window the observer can reach, though still before the world comes back round. Then the dependence is real and forced by the dynamics, but no accessible record shows it. Both the late memory and the resulting failure of chaining stay invisible, so they constrain how such a world can be described, not what the data will show.

   If instead the dependence shows up at a step inside a window the observer does watch, it can actually be detected in their data. The paper's example is three fair, independent bits followed by a fourth equal to the first. An observer whose window ends at step 2 sees no memory. One whose observations reach step 3 can detect it.

6. Cosmological constant: summing the quantum vacuum "jitter" energies gives a number about 120 orders of magnitude bigger than the observed one. In this framework the quantum description belongs to the observer's view, while gravity's source belongs to the underlying world. The paper establishes three things:
   - The zero-point sum and the effective cosmological constant live at different levels of description.
   - Shifting every quantum energy by a constant changes no observable probability, so the vacuum offset appears in no visible statistic.
   - With a Planck cutoff and a Hubble-scale density, the size of the mismatch equals the de Sitter entropy, up to a numerical factor.

   What it proposes, but does not establish: if the gravitational field equations take a certain form, the zero-point sum doesn't cause curvature and nothing needs cancelling. Two completions are consistent with everything visible: one where the offset gravitates and one where it doesn't. Choosing between them needs field equations derived from the underlying world, which is open. The remaining vacuum term isn't computed either, and the observed cosmological constant is simply put in by hand.

   Black-hole information: the framework separates two questions. One is whether information is conserved, which is automatic because the rules are reversible. The other is whether an observer can recover it from accessible records. Small exact computations show these really come apart:
   - Many reversible toy maps keep a message completely hidden from someone who reads only the radiation: 1,376 of 40,320.
   - Some maps that do lose information still let the message be read exactly.
   - Complete hiding requires the unread registers to have at least as many possible values as there are messages.

   What stays open is the Page-curve result. It holds only under three conditions:
   - thorough scrambling, which actually fails for these reversible maps when the black-hole/radiation split matches the configuration coordinates;
   - isolation, which is not established;
   - constant energy, which an evaporating black hole doesn't satisfy.

   The field equations also remain open, as does the link to island and extremal-surface computations.

7. The hardest part was section 2.5 on recurrence and indivisibility. Its idea of a "rooted" forecast table, a hidden prior "shared by every root", rows for zero-probability starts, and "failure somewhere in the cycle" took several readings. The second deck example, with red always showing red and black showing either colour, was described in words only, and I had to rebuild it in my head. I'm not fully sure I got why the overlap appears there.

   I'm also somewhat unconvinced about how much physical bite a failure of chaining has when it may sit only in forecasts from starts that never happen, or beyond any observable window. The paper concedes this, but then it's unclear what testable content remains.

   Section 3.1 on "three notions kept apart" was hard to follow: classical P-divisibility versus quantum CP-divisibility, the Hadamard example, the Pauli-twirl remark. So was the list of Theorem 4's structural assumptions in 3.2, which is dense. Section 3.3, on coherent lifts and right actions, I mostly took on trust. In the black-hole section, the Page-curve conditions and notation, such as the D sector and the effective-pure-state theorem, rely on companion papers I couldn't see.

   More generally, many key steps are "proved on paper" in cited companion papers rather than shown here. So I had to accept them on the paper's authority, and the abstract is much harder to read than the body.
