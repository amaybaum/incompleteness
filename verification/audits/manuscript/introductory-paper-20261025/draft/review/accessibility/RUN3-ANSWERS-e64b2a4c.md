1. **The basic idea.** "Observational incompleteness" means the observer is part of a larger system that runs by exact, reversible, deterministic rules on finitely many states, and the observer can only ever register some of that system's state. The rest is hidden. The paper stresses that hidden means out of reach in principle, not just not yet measured. The paper's own picture is a card game: two cards lie on a table, one face up and one face down, and each shuffle swaps them. A player who sees only the face-up card is in this position. The game's rules contain nothing random, but the player cannot see the whole state, so the game looks uncertain to them. The hypothesis is that every physical observer is in that player's position with respect to the universe.

2. **Randomness from hidden information.** Take the card game. The face-down card was dealt at even odds, so after one shuffle the player cannot predict the colour now showing any better than a coin toss. Nothing random happened during the shuffle. The uncertainty is there because the colour now showing was set by the card the player could not see. More generally, many hidden states fit the same visible state, and the deterministic rule sends them to different visible futures. So from the visible part alone, the next observation looks like a draw from a probability distribution. The paper also gives a very simple general construction. The hidden part stores a whole sequence written in advance, plus a clock, and each step brings the next entry into view. The observer sees a sequence that looks random with exactly the intended odds, even though the system is just reading out a stored list.

3. **The main mathematical result.** Fix a finite number of time steps and a finite set of things the observer can see. Then three collections of observation patterns are exactly the same:
   - all possible probability patterns over sequences of observations (any random-looking record at all);
   - the patterns some finite deterministic, reversible world with a hidden part can show to someone who sees only the visible part;
   - the patterns you get by measuring a finite quantum system in one fixed way at every step, with Born-rule probabilities.

   So any random-looking record can be produced by a hidden deterministic world, and also by a quantum system measured the same way each time. As a result, no record of one repeated measurement can by itself rule out a hidden deterministic explanation. The paper says this is machine-checked in Lean and builds on earlier work by Barandes and by Egri et al.

   "On a finite time horizon" means the match holds over any chosen finite stretch of time, not forever. A finite reversible world eventually returns to its starting state, so one fixed world gives a pattern that repeats. A pattern that never repeats cannot be reproduced exactly for all time by one fixed finite world. You get a world for each horizon, not a single world for all horizons.

4. **What the result does not show.** The result is about possibility, not fact. Because every pattern is in all three collections, the existence of a hidden deterministic model says nothing about any particular law, and nothing about whether nature actually works this way. It also has clear limits:
   - It covers only one fixed measurement repeated at every step. Real quantum mechanics also has incompatible measurements, superpositions and combined systems.
   - It does not single out the "squared amplitude" rule. The quantum systems used are permutations with entries 0 and 1, so any power of the amplitude gives the same table.
   - It does not single out which quantum evolution connects two times.
   - It represents the statistics without giving a physical mechanism.

   To get full operational quantum mechanics, the paper gives five conditions on what an experimenter can do. Examples are that every reversible quantum operation on combined systems is available, and that adding a bystander system changes nothing. With these conditions the available operations are exactly the quantum ones. Observational incompleteness does not imply these conditions: there is a qubit theory that fits the observation set-up but never creates superpositions, so it is not quantum. What remains is to show that a physical hidden world actually supplies conditions (iii)–(v), to find a principle that picks the Born exponent and the evolution between two times, and to deal with Bell correlations. Bell correlations need one lab's hidden response to depend on the other lab's setting, which is the open hypothesis "H-Bell". There is also a separate obstacle: no single fixed finite hidden world can reproduce everything one qubit can do across all experiments, because that would need unbounded hidden memory.

5. **Memory.** A record "has memory" when the next observation depends on more than what is visible now: knowing the past tells you more than knowing only the present. The paper's main point is that whenever the record has memory, every hidden deterministic world that reproduces it must store that memory in its hidden part. The hidden part must also be big enough to hold it. In the card game, the face-down card is what remembers the original deal. Conversely, if even one such world keeps a hidden trace of the past that changes what is seen next, then the record has memory.

   The paper separates two things. A reversible world always stores its whole past, even when the record is memoryless. Memory in this sense is only the part of that store that affects what is seen next.

   What the observer can learn from what they see:
   - The measured memory gives a lower bound on how many states the hidden part must have, roughly 2 to the power of the memory in bits.
   - It also fixes the minimum hidden memory any deterministic completion must carry.
   - If two different pasts lead to visibly different next-step odds, a formula gives a guaranteed minimum amount of memory.

   So observable statistics put limits on something you cannot see directly. There is also a related result about recurrence. Suppose the hidden part starts with the same odds whatever is visible at the start, and some step is not fully predictable. Then somewhere in the world's cycle, the forecasts cannot be built step by step ("P-indivisibility"). This can happen beyond any window the observer can actually access, so the memory might first show up only at very long time scales. One example is a record that looks memoryless for two steps and then repeats the first bit exactly.

6. **The two applications.**
   - **Cosmological constant.** In standard physics, adding up the zero-point "jitter" energy of quantum fields gives a vacuum energy about 120 orders of magnitude bigger than what gravity shows. Here the quantum description is only the observer's description of the visible statistics, while gravity responds to the underlying world's own classical energy. So the two quantities live at different levels of description. What is established: shifting every energy in the quantum description by a constant changes no observable probability, so the zero-point offset does not show up in any visible statistic. What is proposed: if the gravitational field equations take a particular form, the zero-point sum does not curve spacetime, and nothing needs to cancel. What is open: those field equations have not been derived. Without them you cannot decide whether the offset gravitates, because two versions fit the same observations. The leftover vacuum term is not calculated either; the observed value of the cosmological constant is put in by hand. The paper notes that similar ideas already exist, such as trace-free gravity and sequestering.
   - **Black-hole information.** The paper splits the problem into two questions. Is information conserved? Yes, automatically, because the dynamics is reversible. Can an observer recover it from the records they can access? Not necessarily. The analogy is a burned letter: the information is still in the smoke and ash, but nobody watching the smoke can read it. What is established, in small exact toy models:
     - Conservation does not guarantee recovery: 1,376 of the 40,320 reversible maps examined hide the message completely from an observer who reads only the radiation.
     - Conservation is not needed for recovery: 60 non-reversible maps still recover the message exactly.
     - Reversibility guarantees a floor: hiding the message completely needs at least as many possible values in the unread registers as there are messages.

     What is open: the Page-curve result, where the radiation's entanglement rises and then falls back, rests on conditions that are false or unproven here. The scrambling condition fails for these permutation dynamics. The condition that the black hole and its radiation are isolated is not established. And the same-energy condition does not hold for a black hole whose radiation carries away energy. The field equations, the generalized second law and the link to "island" calculations are also open.

7. **Anything unclear.** The hardest part was §2.5 on recurrence and P-indivisibility. I followed the basic point: a forecast built step by step can't turn two overlapping rows into two different certainties. But the role of "rows for starts that never occur", and why a shared hidden prior is needed for the table to make sense, took several passes, and I'm still not fully sure why the failure "somewhere in the cycle" matters physically if it can sit outside any window the observer can access. The difference between memory, indivisibility and quantum non-Markovianity in §3.1 was also dense, especially the Pauli-twirl remark about the card game's memory being "still classical". In §3.2 I took the five conditions roughly on trust; terms like "spectator-independent readout" and "outcome-conditioned composition at every ancilla level" were hard to picture. I was also unsure how much the main theorem says physically. Since it holds for every possible pattern, it seems close to saying "any statistics can be simulated by a lookup table", and the paper itself admits this settles only possibility. The real content seems to be the memory results. The Bell section left me guessing what a "preparation-indexed coupling graph" with long-range edges would look like, and how it could avoid usable signalling. The cosmological-constant section reads more like a reinterpretation that depends on field equations not yet derived than a result. Finally, the many citations to companion papers ("[Main §3.4]", "[GR §6.2]") meant some steps had to be taken on trust from this paper alone.
