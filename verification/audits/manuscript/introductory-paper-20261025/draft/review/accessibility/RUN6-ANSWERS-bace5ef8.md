1. "Observational incompleteness" means being an observer who is itself part of a finite, reversible, deterministic world and can only ever register part of that world's state, the "visible" part. The rest, the "hidden" part, is not just unknown for now; it is out of the observer's reach in principle. The paper's picture is a card game. Two cards lie on a table, one face up and one face down, and every shuffle swaps them. The player sees only the face-up card. The rules are completely fixed, but the player still can't predict what will show next, because part of the state that decides it is face down.

2. The key point is that one visible state can sit alongside several possible hidden states, and those hidden states lead to different futures. The rules are deterministic for the whole world, but the observer only sees the visible part, so from their view the same "now" can go several ways. The randomness is the observer's uncertainty about the hidden part. Nothing random happens in the dynamics. In the card game, after one swap you see whichever card was face down. That card was dealt at random and you never saw it, so it looks like a coin toss. After a second swap the original card returns for certain. My own example: a vending machine that follows a strict internal rule driven by a counter you can't see. To you its output looks random, but it is completely fixed given the counter.

3. Over any fixed, finite number of steps, three sets of possible observation patterns turn out to be the same set:
   - every probability distribution at all over visible histories (every random-looking pattern);
   - every pattern that some finite deterministic, reversible world with a hidden part can show to someone who sees only part of it;
   - every pattern of results from a finite quantum system that evolves unitarily and is measured the same fixed way at every step, with Born-rule probabilities.

   So anything random-looking can be reproduced by a hidden deterministic world, and by a quantum system measured in one fixed basis. One simple construction stores a whole pre-drawn sequence in the hidden register, plus a clock that reveals the next entry each step. The consequence is that no record of one repeated measurement can, by itself, rule out a hidden-deterministic account.

   "On a finite time horizon" means each realization only has to match the pattern for a chosen number of steps. A finite reversible world eventually returns exactly to where it started, so its long-run behaviour is periodic. A non-periodic pattern therefore can't be reproduced forever by one fixed finite world; you get a match per horizon, not for all time.

4. The result proves something is possible, not that it is how nature works. It holds for every pattern, so it can't single out the patterns quantum mechanics actually produces. It also falls short of quantum mechanics in specific ways:
   - It covers only records of one fixed measurement repeated at every step. Real quantum theory also has incompatible measurements, superpositions and composite systems.
   - It doesn't explain why probabilities are the square of the amplitude. That rule is built into the definition of the quantum set, and the permutation-type evolutions used give the same answer for any power.
   - It doesn't pick out which evolution connects two times.
   - It gives a representation, not a physical mechanism.

   To get full operational quantum mechanics, the paper shows that five "completion conditions" are needed. Roughly: probabilities make sense; adding a trivial helper system changes nothing; adding an untouched bystander changes nothing; every reversible quantum operation on combined systems is available; and fresh, evenly mixed helpers can be added, used and discarded. These conditions are necessary and sufficient, and the hidden-world hypothesis alone doesn't imply them. There is an explicit qubit theory without coherence that fits the setup but isn't quantum. Still to be shown: that a real physical substratum supplies conditions (iii)–(v), a principle that selects the squared rule and the evolution between times, and eventually some test that tells this picture apart from standard quantum theory. It is also noted that no single fixed finite hidden world can reproduce everything one qubit can do across all experiments.

5. "Memory" here means the next observation depends on more of the past than just what is visible now. When the record has memory, every hidden deterministic world that reproduces it must store that information in its hidden part, because the visible present by definition doesn't contain it. Conversely, if any one such world keeps a hidden trace of the past that changes what is seen next, the record has memory. The hidden part also has to be big enough. The visible memory can never exceed the hidden memory, and that can never exceed the hidden part's capacity in bits.

   From what they can see, an observer can measure the conditional memory, and that sets lower bounds: a minimum size for the hidden part, and a minimum amount of hidden memory any deterministic completion must carry. If two different pasts lead to the same present but noticeably different forecasts, that sets a measurable floor on the memory. The paper separates simply keeping the past from memory: a reversible world stores its whole past anyway, even for a memoryless record. Memory is only the part of that store that affects what is seen next. The hidden part must also influence what is seen, keep its records, have room for them, and feed them back within the time the observer can see; these are the four conditions C1–C4.

5a. The comparison is meant to tell you something about the world's rule, meaning its dynamics run forward from different visible starts. That only works if everything else is held fixed. If the hidden part were prepared differently depending on the start, the forecasts from two starts would differ partly because of the different preparations, and you couldn't attribute the difference to the dynamics. In the card example, if the dealer put a different face-down card down depending on which card is face up, you'd be comparing two different deals, not one rule started in two ways. A shared, independent hidden setup makes the table of forecasts a property of one physical arrangement.

5b. The result is about the rule, and a rule has to say what happens from every start it could be given, not only from the starts that happen to show up in a given sample. A start that never occurs isn't impossible; the dealer just never chooses it. Including it can matter. In the paper's second deck example, the only thing that stops chaining is an overlap with forecasts from a start that is never dealt, even though the observed game never looks uncertain. The cost, which the paper admits, is that the failure of chaining may then sit only in forecasts no observer will ever see. If every start actually has positive probability, that problem goes away, and the failure becomes equivalent to having memory at some order before the world's return time.

6. **Cosmological constant.** Adding up the zero-point "jitter" energies of quantum fields predicts a vacuum energy about 120 orders of magnitude above what is observed.
   - What the paper establishes: in this picture the quantum description is emergent; it describes the observer's visible statistics, not the underlying world. The zero-point sum belongs to that emergent description, while the effective cosmological constant belongs to the gravitational description of the underlying substratum. Shifting every energy in the quantum description by a constant changes no observable probability, so the offset is invisible in all visible statistics. The two quantities therefore live at different levels.
   - What it leaves open: whether the offset actually sources curvature. That requires covariant gravitational field equations derived from the substratum, which haven't been derived. If they take the targeted form, the mismatch needs no cancellation. Even then the actual value of Λ is not computed; it is taken from observation.

   **Black-hole information.** The usual question is whether radiation from an evaporating black hole carries the information about what formed it.
   - What the paper establishes: it splits that question in two. Is information conserved? Yes, automatically, since the dynamics is reversible. Can it be recovered from what an observer can actually read? That is a separate matter. Exhaustive small toy models show conservation doesn't imply recovery: in 1,376 of 40,320 reversible maps the message stays completely hidden from someone reading only the radiation. They also show conservation isn't needed for recovery: some non-reversible maps recover the message exactly. There is a bound: full hiding needs at least as many unread hidden values as there are possible messages, like secret sharing. The paper's analogy is a burned letter: the information survives in the smoke, but you can't read it.
   - What it leaves open: the Page curve (radiation entropy rising, then falling) is derived only under three conditions, namely thorough scrambling, isolation of black hole plus radiation, and constant energy. The first fails for any reversible map acting on definite configurations with the natural split, the second isn't established, and the third doesn't fit an evaporating black hole. So that part stays conditional and open, as do the field equations.

7. The hardest part was the recurrence and indivisibility section (2.5), with its "rooted transition matrices," "P-divisibility," a hidden prior "shared by every root," and the corollary's interplay of accessible windows, recurrence time and memory at some order below it. The card examples helped. I followed the main logic: a step-by-step forecast can't push two overlapping forecasts apart into two distinct certainties, yet the world must return to certainty when it recurs. But I'm not confident about what the "different deck" example's rule actually is, and I had to guess what the "routed form" of C4 means.

   I also found it hard to see what the indivisibility result tells an actual observer. The paper itself says the failure may lie only in forecasts of starts that never occur, or beyond any window anyone can see, so it seemed more a statement about the model than about data.

   Section 3.1 on three kinds of memory (classical, P-divisibility, CP-divisibility, with Hadamard and Pauli-twirl examples) was dense, and I only got the headline: these are three different properties. The Bell section relies on "ontic parameter dependence" and an open hypothesis "H-Bell" about long-range links in a lattice, which refers to companion papers I couldn't see.

   On how convincing it is: the main equivalence seems almost trivial once you see the construction, a pre-recorded tape plus a clock. The paper concedes this is a statement about what is possible, not about nature. Many key physics pieces (field equations, the Born exponent, which completion conditions hold) are explicitly open, so the applications read as reframings rather than resolutions. Finally, the many citations to the author's own companion papers ("Main," "GR") made it hard to judge, from this paper alone, what is actually proved here.
