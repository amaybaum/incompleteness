\hypertarget{observational-incompleteness-from-deterministic-dynamics-to-quantum-observations}{%
\section{Observational Incompleteness: From Deterministic Dynamics to
Quantum
Observations}\label{observational-incompleteness-from-deterministic-dynamics-to-quantum-observations}}

\textbf{Author:} Alex Maybaum\\
\textbf{Date:} November 2026\\
\textbf{Status:} DRAFT PRE-PRINT\\
\textbf{Classification:} Theoretical Physics / Foundations

\begin{center}\rule{0.5\linewidth}{0.5pt}\end{center}

\hypertarget{abstract}{%
\subsection{Abstract}\label{abstract}}

Could quantum randomness arise because an observer sees only part of a
deterministic world? Observational incompleteness (OI) investigates this
possibility for embedded observers with finite resolution. In finite
reversible models, every probability pattern over a finite observation
window has a hidden deterministic realization, including the records of
repeated fixed-basis quantum measurements. When the observed past helps
predict the future beyond what the present reveals, every such
realization must retain hidden predictive memory, with a minimum
capacity. Under a common independent preparation of the hidden part,
finite recurrence also forces a failure of step-by-step forecasting
whenever a visible transition is uncertain. These results constrain
representations of observations. Reproducing the full range of quantum
experiments additionally requires a physical account of preparations,
measurement choices, state changes and combined systems. The Born rule
and that operational construction remain open. Vacuum energy and
black-hole information illustrate further questions about the relation
between global dynamics and accessible observations.

\hypertarget{could-quantum-randomness-reflect-incomplete-observation}{%
\subsection{1. Could quantum randomness reflect incomplete
observation?}\label{could-quantum-randomness-reflect-incomplete-observation}}

Quantum theory predicts the results of measurements only as
probabilities. Whether that randomness belongs to nature, or shows that
something is missing from the description, is one of the oldest
questions about the theory. Einstein's objection is usually paraphrased
as ``God does not play dice.''

This paper examines a different picture: God plays cards. A deck evolves
by fixed rules and nothing in the rules is random, yet a player who sees
only some of the cards finds the game uncertain. The picture is an
analogy. It shows how hidden information, deterministic rules and
random-looking observations fit together; it proves nothing about
quantum mechanics, and none of the results below depends on it.

Here is the simplest version. Two cards lie on a table, one face up and
one face down. The face-up card is red or black. The face-down card is
dealt at random: it is red or black with equal probability,
independently of the face-up card. Each shuffle is a fixed move, not a
randomization: it swaps the two cards. After one shuffle the player can
predict the colour now showing no better than by tossing a coin. After a
second shuffle the original card is back, with certainty. The player's
record therefore has memory: what is seen next depends on what was seen
earlier, not only on what is showing now. That memory is kept in the
face-down card. Once the cards are dealt nothing random happens; the
uncertainty is the player's, and it comes from what the player cannot
see. The game's statistics are those of ordinary classical probability,
so it illustrates how hidden information works; it is not a model of
quantum mechanics {[}Book §1.11{]}.

Framed this way, a question about interpretation becomes three
mathematical questions. Which patterns of observations can a partly
hidden deterministic world produce? Where must the hidden information
sit? What does storing it force the observations to look like?

\textbf{What is established and what is open.} Section 3 explains the
finite-record correspondence, the hidden memory it requires, and the
consequence of recurrence for forecasts. Section 4 asks what a single
physical model would need to reproduce quantum experiments across
different preparations and measurement choices. Section 5 applies the
distinction between global dynamics and accessible observations to
vacuum energy and black-hole information. Section 6 identifies the
remaining physical constructions.

\hypertarget{the-simple-idea-a-hidden-deterministic-world-can-look-random}{%
\subsection{2. The simple idea: a hidden deterministic world can look
random}\label{the-simple-idea-a-hidden-deterministic-world-can-look-random}}

Start with a world whose rule is \emph{reversible}: each complete
configuration has exactly one successor, and no two configurations have
the same successor. Such a rule loses no information, so the whole
present determines the whole past. In the formal papers this rule is a
\emph{bijection} of the world's configurations.

Now put an observer inside that world. The observer registers only part
of each configuration, the \emph{visible} part; the rest is
\emph{hidden}. Two configurations that look the same to the observer can
differ in their hidden parts and so lead to different visible futures.
The visible part therefore changes in a way that looks random to the
observer, although the whole world changes without any randomness. What
the observer sees depends on three things: the rule, the division into
visible and hidden parts, and how the hidden part is prepared at the
start {[}Main §1.2{]}.

\emph{Observational incompleteness} (OI) is the physical hypothesis that
every physical observer is in this situation: only a part, never the
whole, of a larger world that evolves deterministically and reversibly,
with a specified way of reading and preparing its surroundings {[}Main
§1.2{]}. The finiteness in the hypothesis belongs to the observer:
having finitely many distinguishable internal states, an observer can
tell apart only finitely many visible configurations {[}Main §1.2, Lemma
1{]}. The hypothesis does not require the whole world to be finite
{[}Main §1.2, Remark (status of the early commitments){]}.

The results about hidden worlds in sections 3 and 4 are proved for
\emph{finite models}, called \emph{substrata}. In a finite model the
whole world has finitely many configurations, each a pair of a visible
part and a hidden part, and the rule is a reversible map of those pairs.
Finiteness of the whole is an assumption of these models, not a
consequence of the observer being embedded in the world. The reversible
rule is the models' representative of the hidden dynamics, a choice
motivated by the observed near-unitarity of physical evolution (unitary
evolution, in quantum theory, loses no information) {[}Main §1.2, Remark
(status of the early commitments){]}.

What does the substratum itself run on? The old story of a world resting
on turtles all the way down asks that question. A finite deeper layer
that is prepared independently and evolves on its own, with no coupling
to the substratum, can be added without changing anything this observer
sees {[}Substratum, Theorem 24(iii){]}; a deeper layer that can act back
may change later records. These results neither classify every possible
deeper level nor show that the regress ends. A finite reversible world
is the model under investigation, not a demonstrated final description
of nature {[}Substratum §§2--4; Book §3.8{]}.

Hidden does not mean unknowable. In the two-card game the first two
colours the player sees already identify both cards, even though the
record has memory. In general, for an observer who knows the rule and
how the world was prepared, a record of past observations identifies a
hidden quantity exactly when every starting configuration compatible
with that record gives it the same value {[}Substratum, Prop. 24.5{]}.
Whether hidden information can be inferred depends on the records kept
and on what the observer can do; the result concerns inference from
records and supplies no physical mechanism for storing, preparing or
acting on them.

Nor is this incompleteness the uncertainty principle of quantum theory.
It arises even when the observer always reads the world in one fixed
way, and the visible statistics of such a fixed readout are subject to
no uncertainty relation {[}Main §3.4{]}.

The question of section 1 can now be stated precisely. Fix a number of
steps. A \emph{pattern} lists the probability of every possible sequence
of visible observations over those steps. A hidden world \emph{realizes}
a pattern if, with its rule and its preparation of the hidden part, the
observer's sequence of observations has exactly those probabilities.

\hypertarget{what-the-mathematics-proves}{%
\subsection{3. What the mathematics
proves}\label{what-the-mathematics-proves}}

For finite models, the three questions of section 1 have the following
answers.

\hypertarget{every-finite-pattern-can-be-reproduced-by-a-hidden-deterministic-world}{%
\subsubsection{3.1 Every finite pattern can be reproduced by a hidden
deterministic
world}\label{every-finite-pattern-can-be-reproduced-by-a-hidden-deterministic-world}}

Fix a finite set of possible visible observations and a finite number of
steps. Three collections of patterns can be compared {[}Main §3.4{]}:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\mathsf S\), every pattern at all: every way of assigning
  probabilities to the possible sequences of observations;
\item
  \(\mathsf D\), the patterns a finite reversible world shows to an
  observer who sees only its visible part, the hidden part being of any
  finite size and prepared in any way, possibly correlated with the
  first visible observation;
\item
  \(\mathsf Q_{\rm fb}\) (fb for ``fixed basis''). A \emph{basis} is the
  set of mutually exclusive states that a measurement distinguishes;
  \emph{unitary} evolution is quantum theory's reversible evolution; the
  \emph{Born rule} assigns the outcomes' probabilities.
  \(\mathsf Q_{\rm fb}\) is the set of patterns of results of a finite
  quantum system that evolves by a unitary and is measured in one fixed
  basis at every step, starting from a random mixture of basis states (a
  state with definite probabilities in that basis); the record may be
  any fixed function of each result.
\end{itemize}

Over any finite number of steps the three collections are the same
{[}Main §3.4, Theorem (finite-horizon stochastic--reversible--unitary
equivalence){]}: \[
\mathsf S\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\mathsf D\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\mathsf Q_{\rm fb}.
\]

The statement holds for each finite stretch of time separately, and it
concerns records of one fixed measurement repeated at every step. It
does not say that the rest of quantum theory, with its incompatible
measurements, superpositions and composite systems, is reproduced;
section 4 explains what that would take. Within that scope, a
fixed-measurement quantum record carries no statistical signature that a
partly hidden deterministic world could not reproduce, and conversely.
This settles a question of possibility, not of fact: it does not show
that nature is such a world.

One construction is simple to picture: the hidden part holds a whole
sequence of observations drawn in advance from the pattern, together
with a clock, and each step brings the next entry into view while the
rule stays reversible {[}Main §3.4; EGGH26, Prop. 13{]}. Every pattern
also has a realization in which the hidden part is prepared
independently of the first visible observation, the preparation assumed
in section 3.3 {[}Main §3.4, Theorem (finite-horizon process dilation)
and its Extension{]}. A form of this without reversibility is due to the
same authors {[}EGGH26, Prop. 16{]}, and for Markov chains, processes
without memory, its one-step case is the random-mapping representation
{[}LP17, Prop. 1.5{]}.

On the quantum side, a reversible rule acts on quantum basis states as a
permutation, a relabelling of states, which is a unitary; measuring in
that basis at every step reproduces the visible statistics. Conversely,
the Born rule turns every such quantum record into a pattern {[}Main
§3.4{]}. A permutation dynamics is a special case of Barandes'
unistochastic processes, whose transition probabilities are the squared
magnitudes of the entries of a unitary {[}Bar23; Bar25; Bar26{]}, and
the probabilities of a record measured at every step are those of
Strasberg and García Díaz {[}SGD19{]}.

Two limits follow at once. Patterns without memory, in which the next
observation depends only on the present one, belong to all three
collections, so the existence of a hidden deterministic world
reproducing a pattern does not distinguish patterns with memory from
patterns without it {[}Main §3.4, Remark (what this equivalence does and
does not say){]}. And one fixed finite world eventually repeats itself,
so its record is periodic: the correspondence holds for any chosen
finite stretch of time, but a non-periodic pattern has no exact
realization for all times by one fixed finite world {[}Main §3.4{]}.

\hypertarget{memory-must-be-stored-out-of-sight}{%
\subsubsection{3.2 Memory must be stored out of
sight}\label{memory-must-be-stored-out-of-sight}}

A record has \emph{memory} at a given step when the observed past says
something about the next observation beyond what the present observation
already says. In the card game, what will be seen after the second
shuffle still depends on the colour first showing, although that card is
face down after the first shuffle: the face-down card holds it.

The same holds in every hidden deterministic world that reproduces a
record. When observations contain information about the past that cannot
be recovered from the present observation alone, a deterministic model
reproducing those observations must retain the relevant information in
its hidden state. Precisely, in every such world {[}Main §3.4, Theorem
(unavoidable hidden predictive memory){]}:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  the next observation depends on the past only through what the past
  reveals about the hidden state;
\item
  two pasts can make different predictions only if they leave different
  odds on the hidden state, and their predictions differ by no more than
  those odds do;
\item
  the memory visible in the record, measured in bits, never exceeds the
  memory held in the hidden state, which never exceeds the hidden part's
  capacity, the base-2 logarithm of its number of states.
\end{itemize}

A record has memory if and only if the hidden worlds that reproduce it
keep a trace of the past that changes what is seen next: if the record
has memory, every such world keeps one; if even one such world keeps
one, the record has memory {[}Main §1.5; Main §3.4, Remark (what this
strengthens){]}.

Here \emph{predictive memory} means information about the past that
helps predict the future beyond what the present observation reveals. A
reversible world retains its whole visible past in its complete state,
including for records whose next observation depends only on the
present. Only the part relevant to future predictions contributes to the
observable memory discussed here {[}Main §1.5{]}.

Memory also fixes a minimum size. The hidden part needs at least
\(2^{I}\) states, where \(I\) is the largest amount of information, in
bits, that the observed past carries about the observed future once the
present is known {[}Main §3.4, Theorem (C3 necessity){]}. At each step,
the least memory that a deterministic hidden world, not required to be
reversible, must carry is exactly the information the past carries about
the future once the present is known {[}Main §3.4, Remark (a variational
question){]}. Observed memory thus bounds something that cannot be
observed directly.

Four conditions on the hidden part summarize what produces observable
memory: it must influence what is seen, keep what it records, have room
to record it, and feed the record back into what is seen {[}Main
§1.3{]}. The first three are not enough: a fair coin can be reproduced
by hidden worlds that influence, keep and have ample room, yet never
read anything back, and its record has no memory {[}Main §2.3, Remark
(why (C4) is a separate condition){]}.

\hypertarget{recurrence-and-the-limits-of-step-by-step-forecasting}{%
\subsubsection{3.3 Recurrence and the limits of step-by-step
forecasting}\label{recurrence-and-the-limits-of-step-by-step-forecasting}}

In the card game, the forecast after one shuffle is the same coin toss
whether the first visible card was red or black. After two shuffles,
however, the original colour returns with certainty. A single forecast
rule acting only on the colour after the first shuffle cannot recover
which original colour to predict. The two identical probability
forecasts have lost a distinction that the later observations recover.

This is a failure of \emph{P-divisibility}, the ability to connect
forecasts from a fixed starting time by intervening probability maps.
Such maps cannot make two probability distributions easier to
distinguish. When forecasts from different starts overlap and later
return to different certainties, no intervening map connects them. This
is the contraction argument used in the divisibility literature
{[}RHP14, Thm 2.4; BD16{]} and in the concurrent analysis of Doukas
{[}Dou26, Prop. 1{]}. It also connects the question to Barandes'
stochastic--quantum correspondence {[}Bar23; Bar25{]}.

For the OI result, compare every possible visible start, preparing the
hidden part with the same distribution independently of that start. One
rule and one preparation then determine the whole family of forecasts.
In a finite reversible world, all configurations eventually return to
their starting values. If a visible step is uncertain, the family must
fail to chain somewhere before this return {[}Main §2.3, Theorem
(history readback + finite recurrence implies indivisibility){]}.

The time at which this happens matters. The return may lie far beyond
the observer's experimental window, and the failure can involve starts
absent from the actual preparation. With positive probability for every
visible start, failure somewhere in the cycle is equivalent to
predictive memory somewhere before the return. Neither statement
guarantees memory during a short experiment {[}Main §2.3, Scope{]}.
Three independent fair coin tosses followed by a repeat of the first
illustrate the delay. The hidden world must retain the first toss, but
an observer who stops after three tosses sees no predictive memory
{[}Main §3.4, Remark (a variational question){]}.

On a short window, predictive memory and failure of chaining can also
separate. Two fair tosses followed by their parity have one bit of
predictive memory, although the forecasts chain throughout those three
steps. A finite reversible realization with the common independent
hidden preparation still fails to chain somewhere in its full cycle. The
proof and the window distinction are given in {[}Main §2.3, Scope{]},
with the parity example in {[}RHP14, Eq. (15){]} up to flipping the last
bit, and concurrently in {[}Dou26, App. A{]}.

\hypertarget{what-the-mathematics-does-not-prove}{%
\subsection{4. What the mathematics does not
prove}\label{what-the-mathematics-does-not-prove}}

The correspondence of section 3.1 represents fixed-measurement
statistics in hidden deterministic form. Several things lie outside it.

\hypertarget{representation-is-not-derivation}{%
\subsubsection{4.1 Representation is not
derivation}\label{representation-is-not-derivation}}

The correspondence does not select the Born rule. In the quantum
collection the rule that probabilities are squared magnitudes of
amplitudes (the complex numbers quantum theory assigns to outcomes) is
part of the definition. The unitaries that represent reversible worlds
are permutations; since each entry is 0 or 1, raising the magnitudes to
any positive power, not only the square, gives the same statistics, and
nothing selects the square {[}Main §3.4, Remark (what this equivalence
does and does not say); Bar26, Thm 3.1{]}.

It does not select the quantum evolution between two times. Two quantum
descriptions can reproduce the same visible record at every time and
still differ in the evolution relating two times, by a time-dependent
transformation the record cannot see; choosing between them needs a
principle beyond representability {[}Main §3.4, Remark (scope:
representability, not selection of the relating evolution){]}.

The correspondence establishes that finite observation patterns admit
both representations. A physical explanation must additionally identify
a mechanism that selects the observed pattern and makes its preparation
and readout available. The memory bounds constrain any faithful
deterministic realization once that pattern is specified. Calvo states a
derivation of textbook quantum axioms from indivisible stochastic
evolution {[}Cal26{]}, a further claim beyond the correspondence
described here.

Finally, memory in a record, failure of chaining and memory effects in
the quantum description itself (non-Markovian quantum evolution) are
distinct properties, and no unconditional implication among them is
asserted {[}Main §3.2, Remark (three notions, kept apart){]}. For the
quantum description of a reversible world whose hidden part starts with
definite probabilities, failure of chaining in the record implies
failure of quantum chaining, the same failure when each intermediate
step must be a valid quantum operation {[}Main §3.2, Theorem
(CP-indivisibility, framework scope){]}; outside that class it need not
{[}Main §3.2, Remark (scope: P-indivisibility is not quantum
non-Markovianity){]}.

\hypertarget{what-full-quantum-theory-would-need}{%
\subsubsection{4.2 What full quantum theory would
need}\label{what-full-quantum-theory-would-need}}

Quantum mechanics specifies the probabilities of experiments with
different measurement choices, their effects on later measurements, and
the behaviour of combined systems. The target for OI is operational
equivalence over a stated experimental scope. One realization and one
physical interface must work across preparations, settings, sequences
and combined systems. Every quantum experiment in that scope must be
available, and every admitted OI experiment must agree with its quantum
counterpart.

This target concerns what an embedded observer can prepare, change and
observe. The underlying dynamics may remain deterministic. Hidden
information may contribute through a later interaction without first
being read separately. The required phase-sensitive behaviour and
outcome-conditioned state changes must be obtained from physically
available preparations and interventions, with accessible records and
failed attempts included in the comparison.

Take as given that states are described by matrices of complex numbers,
operations by linear maps on them, and two systems together by the
tensor product of their descriptions. That setting is assumed, not
derived from OI, and a derivation of it is open {[}GR §3.3{]}. Suppose
also a few basic rules: the experimenter can leave the system alone,
lump outcomes together, combine operations, prepare a helper, read the
helper without disturbing the system, and discard it. Within that
setting, the operations available on a finite system, with any finite
helper attached, are exactly those of quantum theory if and only if five
conditions hold {[}GR §3.3, Theorem (operational-completion
characterization){]}:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  probabilities make sense: operations keep states valid and their
  outcome probabilities sum correctly;
\item
  adding a trivial, one-state helper changes nothing;
\item
  an untouched, independent bystander can be added without changing an
  operation;
\item
  \emph{full reversible control}: every reversible quantum operation on
  every combined system is available;
\item
  a fresh helper in a completely random (evenly mixed) state can be
  attached to a combined system, used and discarded, so that a combined
  system can serve as the working system of a larger experiment.
\end{enumerate}

The first two only make the theory well formed; the last three are the
substantive requirements. Each condition is independent of the other
four and of the observation process itself. A \emph{qubit} is the
simplest quantum system, with two basic states; for one fixed finite
observation process with the four properties listed in section 3.2, and
for each condition, there is a qubit theory that realizes the process
and the other four conditions but fails that one. In particular, a
theory that never creates coherence, that is, superpositions, realizes
the observation process and is not quantum, so OI alone does not imply
quantum mechanics {[}GR §3.3{]}. That the five conditions suffice rests
on the standard dilation theorems {[}Sti55; Kra83; Oza84{]}; for
reconstructions of finite quantum theory from operational axioms, see
{[}Har01; CDP11; MM11{]}.

A finite reversible world does not supply condition 4 on its own. The
operations that reversible dynamics of finitely many states supplies,
under the observer access stated in {[}GR §3.3{]}, cannot produce a
quarter-turn relative phase (a phase factor \(i\) on one state), which
full reversible control requires; so condition 4 fails for them on every
system with two or more states {[}GR §3.3{]}. Whether the physical
substratum supplies further kinds of intervention that would complete
its repertoire, in particular a continuously driven transition between
two states, together in some formulations with a selectable relative
phase {[}GR §3.3{]}, and why its operations should be complex matrices
in the first place, are open.

\hypertarget{finite-experiments-and-the-full-range-of-qubit-experiments}{%
\subsubsection{4.3 Finite experiments and the full range of qubit
experiments}\label{finite-experiments-and-the-full-range-of-qubit-experiments}}

The correspondence in section 3.1 chooses a hidden world for each finite
pattern. A qubit admits an indefinitely growing family of preparation
and measurement choices. These are different demands on a model.

A fixed finite world with \(N\) configurations produces
prepare-and-measure statistics through distributions over at most \(N\)
underlying states {[}Main §3.4, Proposition (finite-substratum
operational obstruction --- classical-dimension form){]}. Reproducing
qubit statistics requires classical memory beyond every fixed bound as
the finite experiments grow {[}HKLP24, Thm 4; GH03{]}. Montina reports
the corresponding result of Hardy that the underlying state set cannot
be finite {[}Mon08, citing Har04{]}.

A finite-model comparison therefore has to specify its experimental
scope and resources. Enlarging that scope may require a larger hidden
world. Choosing a separate world for each observed pattern supplies no
common physical interface for all those experiments. An exact account of
the entire qubit family must address the finite-resource obstruction.
Section 2's distinction between the observer's finite resolution and the
size of the whole world leaves that broader construction open.

\hypertarget{bell-correlations}{%
\subsubsection{4.4 Bell correlations}\label{bell-correlations}}

Two laboratories far apart share a prepared pair of systems. Each
chooses a measurement setting and records an outcome \(+1\) or \(-1\).
Bell's theorem constrains explanations in which each outcome depends
only on the local setting and shared information, and that shared
information is independent of the setting choices {[}Bel64{]}. With an
outcome recorded in every run, the combination \(S_{\mathrm{CHSH}}\) of
four setting-pair correlations has magnitude at most 2 {[}CHSH69{]}.
Quantum mechanics reaches \(2\sqrt2\).

The framework's own version is this {[}Main §3.3, Theorem (Bell ceiling
for a screened local completion){]}. Suppose the hidden world is
deterministic; each laboratory's setting acts only on its own region;
each outcome is read from a region that the other laboratory's setting
cannot influence within the time of the experiment; the settings are
chosen independently of the hidden state that precedes them; and an
outcome is recorded in every run. Then \(|S_{\rm CHSH}|\le2\). A
two-player version of the card game obeys the same ceiling when every
operation reads and changes only one player's own cards and depends only
on those cards, that player's setting and chance that, together with the
deal, is independent of the settings; when both players record an
outcome in every run; and when the settings are chosen independently of
the deal {[}Book §1.11; Main §3.3{]}. Reproducing quantum Bell
correlations therefore requires giving up at least one of these
assumptions.

OI keeps determinism and an outcome in every run. It also keeps the
independence of the settings, as an assumption about initial conditions,
and accepts instead that one laboratory's hidden response may depend on
the other laboratory's setting, with no usable signal between them. The
particular substratum the companion papers study is a cubic lattice
whose sites are updated from their nearest neighbours {[}SM{]}; that
update cannot supply the dependence for laboratories outside each
other's range of influence {[}Main §3.3, Corollary (Bell--lattice
obstruction and state-dependent escape){]}. The substratum's coupling
graph records which sites directly affect which. Whether a Bell
preparation can add long-range links to it, with each site gaining only
a bounded number of them, or an equivalent nonlocal response, so as to
supply this dependence while keeping operational no-signalling and the
large-scale continuum limit on which the framework's account of gravity
rests, is an open hypothesis, called H-Bell {[}Main §3.3; Substratum
§3.2{]}.

\hypertarget{why-it-matters}{%
\subsection{5. Why it matters}\label{why-it-matters}}

\hypertarget{quantum-foundations}{%
\subsubsection{5.1 Quantum foundations}\label{quantum-foundations}}

Because every fixed-measurement pattern can be reproduced by a hidden
deterministic world, no record of one repeated measurement can by itself
rule out a partly hidden deterministic account of it. The informative
content lies in what such accounts must contain: where memory is stored,
how much room it needs, and what recurrence does to forecasts.
Deterministic systems have been analysed with quantum methods before,
for example by 't Hooft {[}tH14{]}.

The physical question is which observation patterns arise from one
specified dynamics, preparation mechanism and observer interface. The
finite-record correspondence makes deterministic representations
available. The memory bounds constrain what those representations must
retain. Selecting the quantum family of experiments requires the
operational construction of section 4, including its measurement choices
and composite systems.

\hypertarget{the-energy-of-empty-space}{%
\subsubsection{5.2 The energy of empty
space}\label{the-energy-of-empty-space}}

Quantum fields have zero-point energy even in their lowest states.
Summing it down to a microscopic cutoff gives a density that can exceed
the observed vacuum energy density by roughly 120 orders of magnitude,
depending on the cutoff convention {[}Wei89{]}. The observed density is
represented by the cosmological constant and drives accelerated
expansion. If the zero-point sum gravitated directly, a cancellation of
comparable precision would be needed.

OI separates the quantum description of observations from the
gravitational description of the substratum. A constant shift of quantum
energy changes no visible probability. Within that description, the
absolute zero-point offset is therefore inaccessible {[}GR §6.2{]}. A
gravitational source can nevertheless depend on quantities absent from
these observation statistics.

The proposal in {[}GR{]} is to source gravity from the substratum's
classical energy and momentum. If the targeted field equations follow,
the quantum zero-point sum contributes no curvature. The remaining
vacuum term is uncomputed and the observed cosmological constant remains
an input. The decisive question is the source coupling. Gravitational
completions in which the offset does or does not gravitate can share the
same substratum, observer partition and visible transition law. Deriving
the field equations and their sources is therefore required to decide
between them {[}GR §6.2, §8.5{]}. Related approaches include trace-free
Einstein equations {[}EvEMU11{]}, vacuum-energy sequestering {[}KP14{]}
and Volovik's account of vacuum energy {[}Vol06{]}.

\hypertarget{black-hole-information-keeping-versus-showing}{%
\subsubsection{5.3 Black-hole information: keeping versus
showing}\label{black-hole-information-keeping-versus-showing}}

The black-hole information problem asks whether the radiation of an
evaporating black hole carries the information of what formed it
{[}Haw76; Pag93b{]}. In OI two questions separate: whether information
is conserved by the global dynamics, which holds because the dynamics is
reversible, and whether it can be recovered from the records an observer
can access {[}HP07{]}. A burned letter is a useful analogy: the smoke,
ash and heat still encode its words, because the underlying laws lose
nothing, yet nobody who sees only the smoke can read it.

Unpublished finite register models make this distinction precise
{[}BHa{]}. Registers pass from an interior to a radiation side, and the
observer reads on a fixed schedule. The study ranges over reversible and
irreversible maps supplied as toy models. Some reversible maps keep a
message hidden from radiation-only records, while some irreversible maps
permit exact recovery when all registers are read. Reversibility
guarantees recovery from the complete final state. For a uniform
message, complete hiding also requires at least as many joint values of
the unread registers as there are messages, a capacity bound related to
perfect secret sharing {[}Sha79{]}.

Recovering the quantum Page curve, the rise and fall of radiation
entanglement entropy, requires further structure. The cycle-averaged
argument in {[}GR, App. A{]} assumes typical quantum entanglement along
the dynamics (H-scramble), decoupling (H-decouple), and an energy shell
containing all joint configurations. Under its stated preparation of
definite configurations and its configuration split into interior and
exterior, a reversible permutation keeps each state a definite,
unentangled configuration. H-scramble has no instances in that setting,
so the argument establishes no Page curve {[}GR §8.4, App. A.7--A.9;
BHb{]}. A physical recovery model needs a preparation or partition that
supports the required observable coherence, an attainable scrambling
condition and an appropriate entropy statement. H-decouple and the
smaller energy shell of an evaporating black hole also remain open.

\hypertarget{what-remains-open}{%
\subsection{6. What remains open}\label{what-remains-open}}

Turning mathematical representability into a complete physical theory
needs the following, none of which is established:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Operational quantum theory.} How the complex-matrix and
  tensor-product description of section 4.2 arises physically, and
  whether the substratum supplies the interventions that conditions 3--5
  require {[}GR §3.3{]}.
\item
  \textbf{Selection principles.} A reason for the Born rule's squares,
  and for the evolution relating two times (section 4.1).
\item
  \textbf{Observable memory.} A physical mechanism for memory to affect
  observations at the cosmological horizon, where {[}GR §2.1--2.2{]}
  places the observer's boundary, and experimentally accessible
  consequences of that mechanism {[}Main §4.5{]}. Within a scope of
  exact operational equivalence, OI and QM predict the same observation
  probabilities. A distinguishing test would have to address a
  particular OI model's additional restrictions or behaviour outside
  that equivalence scope.
\item
  \textbf{Dynamical construction.} Joining the realizations of section
  3.1 for successive stretches of time exactly into one world for
  recurrent multi-time patterns; approximating a target over short
  stretches by one fixed substratum {[}Main §3.4{]}; and the
  hidden-sector mixing assumption under which observed memory gives a
  lower bound on how long the hidden part takes to settle {[}Main §1.3,
  §3.4{]}.
\item
  \textbf{Bell correlations.} The hypothesis H-Bell of section 4.4
  {[}Main §3.3; Substratum §3.2{]}.
\item
  \textbf{Gravity.} Covariant field equations derived from the
  substratum, with their coupling to sources, on which the vacuum-energy
  proposal of section 5.2 and the gravitational Page-curve argument both
  depend {[}GR §6.2, §8.5{]}.
\item
  \textbf{Black holes.} A physical preparation and observer partition
  must support the required observable coherence, with a scrambling and
  entropy statement beyond the finite register models. The decoupling
  condition H-decouple is not established: the effective-pure-state
  result of {[}GR, App. A.7{]} concerns the exterior and interior
  jointly, and only for times short compared with the cosmological
  timescale. An evaporating black hole whose radiation carries away its
  energy occupies an energy shell smaller than the set of all joint
  configurations, and that case is also open. So are the entropy along a
  single trajectory, the generalized second law (that total entropy
  including black-hole entropy does not decrease) {[}GR App. A.6{]}, and
  the relation to recent semiclassical calculations of the Page curve
  using quantum extremal surfaces and islands {[}Pen20; AEMM19; AMMZ20;
  Book §7.6{]}.
\end{enumerate}

\hypertarget{where-the-details-are}{%
\subsection{Where the details are}\label{where-the-details-are}}

This paper explains results proved elsewhere and claims no new theorem;
earlier work is credited where it is used. Proofs of the results it
describes are in the formal papers: the finite-horizon correspondence,
hidden memory and its bounds in {[}Main §§1.3, 1.5, 3.4{]}; recurrence
and failure of chaining in {[}Main §2.3{]}; memory, chaining and quantum
non-Markovianity in {[}Main §3.2{]}; the Bell ceiling in {[}Main
§3.3{]}; the operational characterization in {[}GR §3.3{]}; vacuum
energy in {[}GR §6{]}; the Page-curve conditions in {[}GR, App. A{]};
identification from retained records in {[}Substratum, Prop. 24.5{]}.
The formal papers state each result with its full hypotheses and
identify the results they import from other authors.

Several of these proofs are also checked by computer in the Lean 4 proof
assistant with its Mathlib library {[}dMU21; Mathlib20{]}; the formal
papers name the checked declarations beside the statements they support,
and the repository's Lean census and proof-coverage ledger under
\texttt{verification/} record which statement each declaration supports.

The black-hole toy-model results are unpublished finite-model research:
exhaustive enumerations over small register spaces with a proved
recovery floor, whose novelty has not been assessed. Their written
proofs and code {[}BHa; BHb{]} are in
\texttt{verification/programmes/black-hole-information/}, and the finite
probes behind several statements of {[}Main{]} are in
\texttt{papers/oi\_lattice\_code/foundations/}, both released under the
MIT License. The novelty of the operational characterization of section
4.2 and of the result on the evolution relating two times in section 4.1
has also not been assessed.
