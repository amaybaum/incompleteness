## 13.2 The framework's reframing of quantum biology

The conventional treatment of quantum biology has a particular structure. Quantum mechanics is treated as the *exception* in biology — most biological processes are presumed classical, with quantum effects appearing in specific exceptional cases that require special explanation. The exceptional cases include photosynthetic exciton transfer (where quantum coherence persists longer than naively expected), magnetoreception (where radical-pair quantum dynamics may underlie the avian compass), and enzyme catalysis (where quantum tunneling may contribute to hydrogen transfer reactions). Each case is treated as a specific puzzle requiring its own explanation.

The same conditional readback implication applies to each declared observable. Fixed-basis representation by itself supplies neither physical coherence nor a particular mechanism.

Conformational states and chromatin marks are candidate record carriers in the settings discussed in Chapter 12. A test needs to establish which record persists and what later measurement it affects.

Accessible memory is a property of an observable law. A mechanism can be tested against classical models that include additional current-state variables and against quantum dynamical models where relevant.

**Candidate systems.** Photosynthesis, avian magnetoreception and enzyme kinetics suggest different possible records and readouts.

Spectral oscillations, magnetic sensitivity and turnover correlations probe different properties. Each system needs its own comparison between histories at the same present to establish C4.

**Empirical access.** Single-molecule measurements can help define the present observable and follow later transitions. To test readback, the experiment would compare histories at fixed values of that present.

## 13.3 C1–C4 in proteins

A protein's broader conformational landscape is a candidate source of history dependence in a chosen active-site observable.

**The protein partition.** Consider an enzyme catalyzing a reaction. The visible sector is the active site's current configuration — the precise geometric arrangement of catalytic residues, the substrate's binding position, and the local electronic structure that determines the reaction's instantaneous rate. The hidden sector is the broader conformational landscape — the protein's overall fold, the rotameric states of distant side chains, the dynamic network of residue-residue contacts throughout the scaffold, and the bound water structure in the protein's interior.

The partition is *physically meaningful* in the sense that experimentally accessible observables (catalytic rate, binding affinity, fluorescence anisotropy) correspond to visible-sector quantities, while the conformational details determining those observables sit in the hidden sector and are not directly observable at the same time resolution.

**C1: catalytic-conformational coupling.** Active-site dynamics are coupled to the broader conformational landscape through several mechanisms. Substrate binding induces conformational changes in the scaffold (induced fit). Catalysis releases or absorbs energy that propagates through the protein (allosteric coupling). Side-chain rotameric transitions change the active site's electrostatic environment (conformational gating). The coupling is non-trivial: the active site's current behavior depends on the scaffold's current state, and the scaffold's evolution depends on the active site's history of binding and catalysis. C1 is satisfied with substantial coupling strength.

**C2: conformational timescale separation.** Active-site dynamics (substrate binding, bond formation/breaking, proton transfer) operate at $\tau_S \sim 10^{-12}$ to $10^{-9}$ s (picoseconds to nanoseconds). Conformational rearrangements in the broader scaffold operate at $\tau_B \sim 10^{-6}$ to $10^{-3}$ s (microseconds to milliseconds), with some particularly slow conformational transitions taking seconds or longer. The timescale separation is $\tau_S/\tau_B \sim 10^{-9}$ to $10^{-3}$ — substantially smaller than the cosmological case ($10^{-32}$) but comparable to or larger than engineered quantum systems (qubits at $10^{-3}$, atomic clocks at $10^{-6}$).

**C3: conformational landscape capacity.** A coarse conformation count illustrates possible record states. The usable capacity depends on which states persist and can be distinguished through the proposed coupling.

**A matched-present test.** If conformational records influence later active-site dynamics differently for histories sharing the same present, the observable law carries conditional memory.

*Nonexponential kinetics.* Waiting-time distributions can reflect mixtures of states with different rates, including Markov models in the enlarged state.

*Fluctuating rates.* Single-enzyme rates can fluctuate across turnover windows. Lag correlations motivate a conditional test with a specified present state.

*Conformational gating.* Binding, release and some tunneling models depend on molecular conformation. The causal effect of a retained record depends on which transitions and histories are compared.

*Inhibition and activation.* Slow relaxation after allosteric ligand binding can motivate a comparison at matched present measured state.

Empirical work on these signatures includes single-molecule measurements and model comparisons. The readback claim requires the conditional test above.

**Empirical status.** The measured turnover and conformation dynamics motivate tests of specific models. Raw kinetics leave the presence and source of C4 open for the declared readout.

## 13.4 Photosynthesis and exciton–vibration dynamics

Engel and colleagues reported oscillations in two-dimensional electronic spectroscopy of the FMO complex. Distinguishing vibrational, electronic and mixed contributions requires detailed spectroscopy and dynamical modeling ([Complexity §7.4]).

The exciton–vibration interface is a candidate memory architecture. An appropriate dynamical comparison would ask which state is retained and influences later transfer at the same measured present.

**The photosynthetic partition.** In a light-harvesting complex (LH1, LH2, FMO, or others), the visible sector is the *exciton* — the photoexcited electronic state delocalized across multiple pigment molecules (chlorophylls, bacteriochlorophylls, carotenoids). The hidden sector is the *protein-vibrational environment* — the protein scaffold's vibrational modes, the solvent dynamics, and the slow conformational fluctuations that modulate the pigments' electronic energies and inter-pigment couplings.

**Coupling and timescales.** Exciton–vibration coupling motivates a candidate visible and hidden description. Lifetimes and distinguishable vibrational states are model inputs. The C4 contrast needs its own witness.

**Proposed history test.** A vibrational record that changes later exciton-transfer laws at matched present would give conditional memory. Detectable distinguishability revival would require a comparison with specified initial preparations and readout.

Duan et al. report short electronic coherence in their FMO spectroscopy ([Complexity §7.4]). Cao and Plenio model vibronic contributions to transfer. The model and spectroscopy provide separate information about candidate dynamics.

**Empirical implications.** Measurements across controlled vibrational preparations could help resolve electronic and vibronic contributions. The C4 test needs a specified measured state, histories and subsequent observable.

The effectiveness of a candidate transfer mechanism depends on the observed dynamics and a comparison with alternatives.

## 13.5 A radical-pair hypothesis for magnetoreception

The avian magnetic compass — the mechanism by which migratory birds sense the geomagnetic field for navigation — provides a second canonical case of quantum biology. The leading hypothesis (Ritz, Adair, Schulten, and others, from the 1970s through the present) is that magnetoreception uses a *radical-pair mechanism* in cryptochrome proteins in the avian retina: photoexcitation of cryptochrome produces a spin-correlated radical pair whose recombination yield depends on the geomagnetic field through Zeeman splitting of the radical-pair states.

The radical-pair model motivates a test of whether nuclear and molecular degrees of freedom retain information relevant to recombination.

**The radical-pair partition.** A radical pair consists of two unpaired electrons in adjacent molecular orbitals, with the two-electron spin state initially correlated (singlet $|S\rangle$ or triplet $|T_0\rangle$, $|T_\pm\rangle$, depending on the photochemical mechanism). The visible sector is the *electron spin state* — the two-electron spin configuration that determines the recombination products. The hidden sector is the *nuclear spin environment* — the hyperfine-coupled nuclear spins of the surrounding nuclei, the molecular vibrations modulating the hyperfine couplings, and the protein scaffold dynamics affecting the radical pair's lifetime.

**Candidate partition.** Hyperfine coupling can connect the electron spins with nuclear spins. Whether a useful record persists, and changes a later yield at fixed present, depends on mechanism-specific rates, controls and readout.

**A proposed history test.** Compare the recombination laws for histories matched at a declared radical-pair observable. The magnetic-field dependence and the existence of a retained record are distinct measurements.

Radical-pair lifetime sets an available reaction interval. It cannot by itself determine the coherence lifetime or the response in a bird.

**Empirical context.** Behavioral tests and in vitro molecular assays inform different parts of the hypothesis.

- *Light dependence.* The light dependence of the avian compass motivates photochemical mechanisms.
- *Inclination sensitivity.* The observed compass response to field orientation constrains candidate mechanisms.
- *Radiofrequency disruption and in vitro sensitivity.* Behavioral radiofrequency results motivate radical-pair models. Xu et al. report magnetic sensitivity of European robin cryptochrome 4 in vitro ([Complexity §7.4]). The responsible receptor and its mechanism in vivo remain open.

**Further tests.** The proposed mechanism needs a link from molecular responses to the sensory system and a declared C4 assay for a retained record. The magnetic sensitivity results alone do not give either.

## 13.6 Enzyme catalysis as the framework's clearest test case

Enzyme catalysis is the framework's most direct empirical test bed in quantum biology. Single-enzyme experiments — pioneered by Sunney Xie and developed by many groups over the past two decades — provide single-molecule resolution of catalytic events, with sufficient time resolution to detect the framework's predicted non-Markovian signatures. This section develops the specific empirical predictions and their match to experimental data.

**The empirical record.** Single-enzyme studies report several kinds of temporal behavior:

*Dynamic disorder.* Individual enzyme molecules exhibit fluctuations in catalytic rate over time. The fluctuations are not random noise but exhibit structured temporal correlations — slow periods (low catalytic rate) cluster together; fast periods (high catalytic rate) cluster together. The autocorrelation function of catalytic rate decays on timescales much longer than individual catalytic cycles, indicating that the rate fluctuations have *memory*.

*Nonexponential waiting-time distributions.* Stretched-exponential and multiexponential fits describe some observed waits. A mixture or projection of Markov states can produce such waits.

*Correlations between cycles.* Successive turnover durations can be correlated. A matched-present history test requires conditioning beyond the raw lag correlation.

*Allosteric history dependence.* Inhibitors and activators binding away from the active site produce activity changes with kinetics dependent on the protein's history. The "memory" is the slow relaxation of the conformational substrate to the new ligand-bound state.

**A conditional readback test.** The readback result identifies memory when histories of the same measured present have different subsequent laws. A biochemical model is required to identify which retained variable accounts for the contrast.

The individual findings motivate tests across specific conformations and histories. The existence, time window and magnitude of accessible memory depend on the given enzyme, perturbation and measurement.

**Quantitative testability.** A parameterized kinetic model could predict waiting-time distributions and correlation functions. The finite-horizon result specifies the conditional contrast without fixing those forms. A separate revival test could address the mechanism proposal in §13.7.

The diagnostic experiments are within reach of current single-molecule techniques: multi-time correlation measurements with sufficient statistics to constrain the algebraic structure of the transition probabilities, performed on well-characterized enzymes with known conformational landscapes. The framework's content here is therefore empirically actionable, with the prediction of the restricted quantum-statistical regime being the mechanism proposal's distinctive falsification target.

## 13.7 The restricted quantum-statistical regime in biology

The framework's content on quantum biology includes a structurally distinct prediction: the mechanism proposal predicts that biological C1–C4 systems lie in the *restricted quantum-statistical regime* of Chapter 18 §18.3, partial distinguishability revival on accessible windows ($0 < \mathcal{N} < 1$) — a revival range, not a degree of quantum membership ([Main §3.4]), and not implied by C1–C4, which give memory without fixing $\mathcal{N}$ ([Main §2.3]). Partial revival itself is standard open-systems phenomenology, quantified in the literature by the Breuer–Laine–Piilo measure; what is distinctive is the proposed capacity-controlled origin and scaling law, the mechanism proposal's prediction for systems with marginal hidden capacity.

**The regime's empirical signature.** C4 is a statement about multi-time laws at fixed present. The measure $\mathcal{N}$ concerns revival of distinguishability in a specified rooted map family. Conditional memory and divisible population laws can coexist on an accessible window ([Main §2.3]). The capacity law and any proposed conditions on operational completion require their own derivation.

**Biological systems in the restricted quantum-statistical regime.** The biological scales developed in §§13.3-13.6 — protein conformational landscapes ($\tau_S/\tau_B \sim 10^{-9}$ to $10^{-3}$), photosynthetic light-harvesting ($\tau_S/\tau_B \sim 10^{-1}$ to $10^{-4}$), magnetoreception ($\tau_S/\tau_B \sim 10^{-3}$), single-enzyme catalysis ($\tau_S/\tau_B \sim 10^{-9}$ to $10^{-12}$) — are slow-bath ratios. Hidden-sector capacities range from $\sim 10^{30}$ (radical pair) to $\sim 10^{200}$ (protein conformational landscape) — large but finite, the finiteness capping accessible memory ([Main §3.4]). Capacities of this size lie far above the comparable-capacity range $N_H/N_V \sim 1$ of Chapter 15 §15.7, so the placement of biological systems in the restricted quantum-statistical regime is a conjecture that the capacity ratio does not fix.

The proposal's conjecture is that *biological systems show partial distinguishability revival (the restricted quantum-statistical regime)* at the C1–C4 scales of interest — a revival claim, not a quantum-membership claim ([Main §3.4]). This is structurally distinct from "biology has quantum effects" (which the framework's reading agrees with) and from "biology is fundamentally quantum-mechanical at all scales" (which the framework's reading rejects). The restricted quantum-statistical regime is the mechanism proposal's specific commitment.

**Empirical access.** Multi-time measurements can estimate the matched-present contrast for a chosen enzyme’s observable. To measure revival, prepare the specified states and track the relevant distinguishability on the same window.

A test of the mechanism proposal needs a declared system, preparations, observation window, conditioning and noise model. A divisible rooted population family is compatible with positive conditional memory.

The proposed capacity dependence would be tested by varying accessible capacity under stated dynamics and measuring revival.

## 13.8 Implications for drug design

Drug-target kinetics can be approximated by a model of current state when that state is sufficient. Additional conformational variables may be needed for some experiments.

**Kinetic implications.** Standard pharmacology characterizes a drug-target interaction with two rate constants: $k_{\text{on}}$ (binding rate) and $k_{\text{off}}$ (dissociation rate), with the equilibrium dissociation constant $K_d = k_{\text{off}}/k_{\text{on}}$ determining the binding affinity. The framework's prediction is that these rate constants are *not constants* but vary with the protein's conformational state — fast periods (high $k_{\text{on}}$, low $k_{\text{off}}$) cluster together; slow periods cluster together. The clinical consequence: a drug's effective potency varies over time in ways the constant-rate-constant model cannot capture.

**Allosteric implications.** Ligands can act through protein conformation. In a system with history-dependent ligand response at matched present, a kinetic model would need to account for the retained variables.

**Resistance mechanisms.** Drug resistance often involves mutations in the target protein that change the active site or the allosteric communication network. The framework's reading: resistance is a *memory-structure alteration* — the mutations change the protein's C1–C4 architecture, producing different non-Markovian dynamics and therefore different drug-target kinetics. The framework's content suggests that resistance can be combatted by designing drugs that exploit specific features of the C1–C4 architecture rather than just optimizing static binding affinity.

**Catalytic vs regulatory specificity.** The framework's structural content distinguishes drugs that target the active site (modulating the fast subsystem) from drugs that target the regulatory landscape (modulating the slow subsystem). The two categories have different kinetic signatures and different clinical implications. Chapter 16's medicine content develops this distinction in detail for specific clinical contexts; the structural foundation is the C1–C4 architecture developed in this chapter.

**The magnitude of corrections.** The size of the framework's corrections to standard drug kinetics is not fixed by $\tau_S/\tau_B$. The ratio sets how long the scaffold retains a record, of order $\tau_B/\tau_S$ active-site events; the size of the correction is set by how strongly that record modulates later events, which is open for protein systems. For drug design, the practical question is whether the cumulative effect reaches clinical relevance at the doses and timescales of therapeutic use. The framework's content provides a structural basis for predicting when corrections matter rather than just empirically discovering them case by case.

## 13.9 Chapter close

Enzyme kinetics, exciton transfer and magnetoreception offer distinct experiments in which to look for a persistent record and compare its effects at matched present.

**Three categories of empirical content.** The chapter's content sorts into three categories.

*Established empirical content.* The cited studies report FMO spectral oscillations, magnetic sensitivity of cryptochrome in vitro and temporal structure in single-enzyme kinetics. Their conclusions use different readouts and do not determine a common C4 mechanism.

*Quantitative model proposals.* Enzyme waiting times, multi-time correlations and temperature responses can be modeled for specified molecular systems. The finite-horizon result supplies the conditional test of accessible memory.

*Predictions for future empirical work.* The restricted quantum-statistical regime's diagnostic signature (distinguishability-revival measurements, with multi-time correlation measurements testing memory) is the mechanism proposal's distinctive falsification target. Engineered biological systems with tunable C1–C4 parameters (designed proteins, synthetic photosynthetic systems, artificial radical-pair platforms) could provide controlled tests of the framework's quantitative predictions.

**Forward pointers.** Chapter 14 gives a BQP theorem under a uniform scalable model with quantum operations, efficient simulation and charged conditioning. Chapter 15 proposes a BEC test of a capacity-controlled revival mechanism, with an estimator and controls still to be specified. Chapters 16 and 17 discuss candidate biomedical applications.
