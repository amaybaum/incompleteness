## 7.3 The emergent action scale $\hbar$

The most distinctive content of the framework's gravitational sector is the treatment of Planck's constant $\hbar$ as an emergent action scale whose relation to the boundary geometry is fixed by the cosmological horizon's thermodynamics. The standard view treats $\hbar$ as a fundamental constant of nature, its value $1.055 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$ measured. The framework derives $\hbar = c^3 \epsilon^2 / (4G\sigma)$ from thermal self-consistency between classical horizon thermodynamics and the emergent quantum field theory on the horizon — with $\sigma$ the entropy of one boundary mode and $\epsilon$ the discreteness scale of the substratum partition, fixed by self-consistency in Section 7.4 to $\epsilon = 2\sqrt{\sigma}\, l_p$; the two relations together fix only the ratio $\epsilon/\sqrt{\sigma} = 2\,l_p$, a self-consistency condition rather than an independent determination of the value of $\hbar$.

The derivation proceeds in four steps.

**Step 1 (classical horizon temperature).** The calibration uses the bifurcate Killing horizon of exact de Sitter, where the event and apparent horizons agree. With the supplied Einstein first-law relation $dE=(c^2\kappa/8\pi G)\,dA$ and one mode of entropy $\sigma$ per area $\epsilon^2$, the classical temperature is
$$k_B T_{\text{cl}} = \frac{c^2 \epsilon^2 \kappa}{8\pi G \sigma}.$$
Under H-count the total system is finite (A1), and the energy-dependent entropy of the conserved energy shell is the boundary count. The counting derivative gives $\beta_E=1/(k_BT_{\rm cl})$. The corresponding matching in dynamical FLRW remains open. [GR §2.3]

**An independent route to Einstein's equations.** Einstein's equations would follow for the framework's emergent matter, given the single-cone structure qualified below, from an external theorem with an entirely different axiom base. The gravitational closure program of Düll, Schuller, Stritzelberger and Wolz (Phys. Rev. D 97, 084036) shows that for matter field equations whose causal structure is that of standard-model fields on a single Lorentzian light cone — the class the framework's emergent sector occupies if its single-cone structure holds, that structure being supported by the rotational-protection results of the companion papers, conditional on loop-level octahedral invariance and an aligned condensate (Chapter 19 §19.3.7), and by the symmetric-point universality of the block cones under H-blind ([SM §4.4]) — the requirement that matter and geometry evolve together consistently, independently of how spacetime is sliced, forces the gravitational action to be Einstein–Hilbert with a cosmological constant. No thermodynamics enters that argument at all. Where the closure route leaves Newton's constant and the cosmological constant undetermined, the framework's relation ℏ = c³ε²/(4Gσ) and the dark-energy analysis of the following sections take over. The closure theorem is, moreover, one of a family of such necessity results that the emergent sector inherits: Lovelock's theorem, which shows that in four dimensions the only second-order, generally covariant dynamics for a metric alone is Einstein's with a cosmological constant, and the spin-2 results of Weinberg and Deser, which show that a massless spin-two particle coupled to a complete matter sector is forced into universal coupling and Einstein's theory at low energy. Each of these routes would deliver Einstein's dynamics from hypotheses the framework has not established — the emergent light cone, the massless spin-two bound state, the absence of extra light gravitational modes — and they answer the standard objection facing every emergent-spacetime program, that such constructions deliver the geometry of spacetime but never Einstein's dynamics, only once those hypotheses are established. The thermodynamic derivation of this chapter uses Einstein's equations through Jacobson's relation and does not derive them; the covariant field equations remain open (Chapter 19 §19.2). The behaviour of the framework's small lattice corrections under the closure machinery remains an open consistency check.

**Step 2: Boundary-only dependence.** The visible transition data from which the emergent quantum field theory on the horizon is reconstructed depend only on the hidden degrees of freedom within the visible sector's causal cone over the elapsed time, a layer whose depth grows with $t$, and not on the deep content of the hidden sector beyond it (Appendix B.4). They depend at leading order only on the boundary modes — the degrees of freedom at the horizon itself — under a posited bound on the deep sector's influence on the boundary layer, H-BD, with influence time $\tau_{BD}$: the corrections are then of order $t/\tau_{BD}$, which is $10^{-32}$ for laboratory processes when $\tau_{BD} \gtrsim 1/H$, a value that is not derived, and for the framework's linear update the bound with an influence time longer than two update steps holds only if the deep sector does not act on the boundary at all ([GR §3.2]). The transition data fix the emergent dynamics in units of inverse time but not the action scale that converts it to energy, so this step does not bear on $\hbar$: with Step 3 it is a consistency check, and the calibration of Step 4 fixes $\hbar$.

**Step 3 (dimensional analysis of $\hbar$).** The cancellation of surface gravity in Step 4 leaves $c$, $\epsilon$ and $G$ as dimensional inputs. Their unique combination with units of action is $c^3\epsilon^2/G$. The normalization follows from the thermal matching.

**Step 4 (thermal self-consistency).** On exact de Sitter the KMS frequency scale is set by the Euclidean period $\tau_K=2\pi c/\kappa$ under the stated horizon and state conditions. The corresponding temperature is $k_BT_Q=\hbar/\tau_K$. The calibration itself uses the infrared slope H-slope.

The frequency slope is matched to the classical energy slope $\beta_E$ for the same operational transitions under H-count. Thus $\beta_E\hbar=\tau_K$, or $T_{\rm cl}=T_Q$ in temperature units:
$$\frac{c^2 \epsilon^2 \kappa}{8\pi G \sigma} = \frac{\hbar \kappa}{2\pi c}.$$
The common surface gravity $\kappa$ cancels. Solving gives
$$\boxed{\hbar = \frac{c^3 \epsilon^2}{4G\sigma}}.$$

The derivation has the structure of a gap equation: $\epsilon$ is the free geometric input, $\hbar$ is the output. H-count supplies the classical energy slope and H-slope the frequency slope for the same operational transitions. The $1/4$ coefficient in Section 7.5 inherits these conditions. One prerequisite of the KMS step is carried explicitly: Euclidean regularity at a bifurcate Killing horizon presupposes local boost structure in the emergent description at the horizon, so the gap equation is partially downstream of the boost sector's protection mechanism: the symmetric-point matching under H-blind ([SM §4.4]) and, conditional on it, the $M_X$-controlled running tracked in the Lorentz-sector accounting ([GR §8.5]). At the normalized $d=3$ observer level the free massless branch supplies the common infrared cone at the symmetric matching point under H-blind ([SM §4.4]); exact all-momentum boost invariance is not claimed — the unit-Courant cone of the one-dimensional discrete wave equation does not carry to the three-dimensional observer branch, and away from the body diagonals the cone holds only in the infrared ([GR §8.5]). Conditional on that matching, the custodial symmetry forbids a condensate-independent sector-differential counterterm, and an interacting cross-charged calculation, reported in the companion paper and not independently verified, gave the running a quadratic-with-logarithm form decoupling as $(M_X/M_{\text{Pl}})^2$, with the non-logarithmic constant that loop's own, the substratum's component-local nonlinearity contributing only to the universal cone. The one item shared with the Lorentz-sector accounting is that reported running calculation; on its reported form the cross-charged mass $M_X$ would sit in an illustrative window of roughly two to four decades, kept open from below by the baryon safety of the cross-charged sector, which is of the proton-safe leptoquark class rather than the grand-unified kind. The gap equation gives $\hbar$ in terms of $\epsilon$ and $\sigma$ on this branch.

**One boundary mode per area $\epsilon^2$, $\eta = \sigma/\epsilon^2$, fixes $\epsilon$; the internal components fix its relation to the lattice.** The entropy-density argument in Step 1 used $dS = \sigma\,dA/\epsilon^2$ — one boundary mode per area $\epsilon^2$. This counting fixes $\epsilon$, the horizon area per boundary mode being $\epsilon^2$, rather than adding an ansatz about the mode density. Two potential multiplicities are settled separately. The number of internal *states* per mode (the alphabet size $q$) does not change the mode count; it bounds the entropy $\sigma$ of a mode, $\sigma \leq \ln q$, and the alphabet-size hypothesis of Chapter 5 does not fix $\sigma$; the numerical values of this chapter rest on the assumption $\sigma = 1$, one nat per boundary mode, which the bound makes unavailable for a single mode with $q = 2$. The Bekenstein-Hawking coefficient does not depend on $\sigma$, which cancels from the entropy law and enters only the relation between $\epsilon$ and the Planck length, $\epsilon = 2\sqrt{\sigma}\,l_p$ ([GR §3.1, §4]). The number of internal *components* per site ($K = 2d = 6$) is physical and enters through the link count. On the single-link reading, in which the $K = 2d$ components are the $2d$ link directions, each coupling across exactly one link (not derived from the substratum dynamics, [SM §4.5]), a horizon patch with unit normal $\mathbf n$ is crossed by $\lvert n_1\rvert+\lvert n_2\rvert+\lvert n_3\rvert$ links per square of the lattice spacing: one for a patch orthogonal to a lattice axis, $\tfrac32$ on average over a sphere. On the spherical horizon, therefore, $\epsilon^2$ is two thirds of the squared lattice spacing, and the lattice spacing is $\sqrt{6\sigma}\,l_p$ ([GR §3.1]); one mode per area $\epsilon^2$ does not establish one nat per mode.

The counting also sits at the intersection of an independent family of results: the Bekenstein bound and Bousso's covariant entropy bound each cap the entropy of a region by its boundary area in Planck units — area-law mode counting reached from quantum field theory and from light-sheet geometry respectively. One caution keeps the logic clean: those bounds are formulated with $\hbar$ already in hand, and the framework's relation for $\hbar$ comes downstream of this counting, so the family can serve only as downstream convergence — the emergent theory, once $\hbar = c^3\epsilon^2/(4G\sigma)$ is fixed, satisfies both bounds — and never as motivation for the counting, which stands on the definitional argument above alone. Citing the bounds as motivation would re-import the circularity that the definitional argument removed.

**The consequences of the gap equation.** Given $\epsilon$, the gap equation fixes $\hbar$; the specific relationship $\hbar = c^3 \epsilon^2 / (4G\sigma)$ then enters three later results. In Section 7.5 it enters the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy $S = A/(4 l_p^2)$ with the factor $1/4$. In Section 7.7 it enters the de Sitter entropy $S_{\text{dS}}$ as the compression ratio of the conditional cosmological constant dissolution. In Section 7.8 it enters the Type II running-vacuum reading of the dark energy. The three relations share the calibration; they are consequences of it, not independent checks of it. The situation is analogous to deriving the Schwarzschild metric with mass $M$ as a free parameter: the derivation has genuine content (the functional form of the metric) even though one input is not determined from within the derivation.

The framework's relation to Jacobson's original derivation deserves explicit comment. Jacobson's argument [Phys. Rev. Lett. 75, 1260 (1995)] uses $\hbar$-containing forms throughout; the present derivation does not — it uses the classical identity $dE = (c^2 \kappa / 8\pi G)\,dA$ and the classical entropy density $\eta = \sigma/\epsilon^2$. The logical ordering differs from Jacobson's: his argument derives $G$ from $\eta$ and $\hbar$; the present argument takes $G$ as a parameter of the classical Hamiltonian and expresses $\hbar$ through $G$ and $\epsilon$; since $\epsilon/\sqrt{\sigma}$ is not determined independently, the relation fixes $\epsilon/\sqrt{\sigma}$ from the measured $\hbar$ (Section 7.4). The two are consistent on the spherical horizon — substituting $\eta = \sigma/\epsilon^2$ and $\hbar = c^3 \epsilon^2/(4G\sigma)$ into Jacobson's relation $G_{\text{eff}} = c^3/(4\eta\hbar)$ recovers $G_{\text{eff}} = G$, with $\sigma$ cancelling — but the roles of input and output are reversed. On a planar patch with unit normal $\mathbf n$ the link density of Section 7.3 gives $G_{\text{eff}}(\mathbf n) = 3G/\big(2(\lvert n_1\rvert+\lvert n_2\rvert+\lvert n_3\rvert)\big)$, so the relation holds for the area-averaged density of the spherical horizon (and on patches with $\lvert n_1\rvert+\lvert n_2\rvert+\lvert n_3\rvert = \tfrac32$), not patch by patch. The Gibbons-Hawking form $k_B T_{\text{GH}} = \hbar \kappa / (2\pi c)$ enters as the KMS temperature of Step 4; the calibration relates the $\hbar$ in it to $\epsilon$ and $\sigma$ through $\hbar = c^3\epsilon^2/(4G\sigma)$.

## 7.4 Self-consistency and the discreteness scale

The gap equation $\hbar = c^3 \epsilon^2 / (4G\sigma)$ from Section 7.3 takes $\epsilon$ as a free geometric parameter — the discreteness scale of the substratum partition — and produces $\hbar$ as the output. The framework's content beyond this single relation requires that $\epsilon$ itself be determined. The determination comes from self-consistency with the definition of the Planck length.

The Planck length $l_p = \sqrt{\hbar G / c^3}$ is defined as the unique length scale constructed from $\hbar$, $G$, and $c$. Substituting the framework's relation $\hbar = c^3 \epsilon^2 / (4G\sigma)$ into this definition:
$$l_p^2 = \frac{\hbar G}{c^3} = \frac{c^3 \epsilon^2}{4G\sigma} \cdot \frac{G}{c^3} = \frac{\epsilon^2}{4\sigma}.$$
Rearranging gives the discreteness scale:
$$\boxed{\epsilon = 2\sqrt{\sigma}\, l_p.}$$

The result is, for a given $\sigma$, the unique value of $\epsilon$ consistent with the framework's gap equation and the standard definition of the Planck length. The horizon area per boundary mode is $\epsilon^2 = 4\sigma\,l_p^2$, so $\epsilon$ is $2\,l_p$, twice the Planck length, at $\sigma = 1$; on the single-link reading of Section 7.3 the lattice spacing is $\sqrt{3/2}\,\epsilon = \sqrt{6\sigma}\,l_p$.

Here $\sigma$ is the entropy of one boundary mode, a dimensionless number of nats, and the Bekenstein-Hawking coefficient of Section 7.5 does not depend on it ([GR §3.1, §4]). The alphabet-size hypothesis of Chapter 5 does not fix $\sigma$: a mode with $q$ internal states carries at most $\ln q$, so the assumption $\sigma = 1$, one nat per boundary mode — an assumption about the entropy of a mode, not a choice of units, since entropy is already measured in nats — is unavailable for a single mode with $q = 2$.

**The self-consistency is algebraically equivalent to the gap equation.** The framework contains one free geometric parameter; self-consistency with the Planck length definition fixes its value for a given $\sigma$. It adds no relation beyond the structural commitment $\hbar = c^3 \epsilon^2 / (4G\sigma)$ and the definition of $l_p$, and its numerical value takes the measured $\hbar$ as input. The result is a *constraint* on $\epsilon$, not an independent measurement.

**The discreteness scale and $\sigma$.** With $\sigma$ undetermined, self-consistency fixes only the ratio $\epsilon/\sqrt{\sigma} = 2\, l_p$, and a Planck-scale $\epsilon$ additionally requires $\sigma$ of order one. The bound $\sigma \leq \ln q$ limits $\sigma$ from above for a mode with $q$ states, but the count bounds it from below only by $\sigma > 0$: $\sigma = 10^{-12}$ would give $\epsilon = 2 \times 10^{-6}\, l_p$. The gap equation does not exclude discreteness scales far from the Planck length; whether they are excluded on independent grounds is open.

The framework's content in the discreteness scale therefore has three structural commitments: the gap equation, the Planck-length definition, and the self-consistency between them. The result $\epsilon = 2\sqrt{\sigma}\, l_p$ is a derived quantity for a given $\sigma$, not a free parameter, with $\epsilon = 2\,l_p$ at $\sigma = 1$, one nat per mode — but the derivation requires accepting the gap equation as the substratum's structural commitment rather than $\hbar$ as a fundamental constant.

## 7.5 The Bekenstein-Hawking entropy

The Bekenstein-Hawking entropy $S = A / (4\, l_p^2)$ assigns each horizon a thermodynamic entropy proportional to its area, with the proportionality constant $1/(4\, l_p^2)$. Bekenstein (1973) introduced the area law with a coefficient he could estimate only heuristically; Hawking (1975) fixed the $1/4$ from the Hawking temperature and the first law, and the semi-classical and stringy microstate arguments reproduce it. The framework reproduces the same ratio from its horizon calibration: with the discreteness scale $\epsilon = 2\sqrt{\sigma}\, l_p$ fixed in Section 7.4 under the condition H-slope, the boundary-mode count gives the area law with the coefficient $1/4$.

**Direct mode counting.** With $\epsilon = 2\sqrt{\sigma}\, l_p$ from Section 7.4, the number of independent boundary modes on a horizon of area $A$ is
$$N_{\text{modes}} = \frac{A}{\epsilon^2} = \frac{A}{4\sigma\, l_p^2}.$$
Each area $\epsilon^2 = 4\sigma\, l_p^2$ of the horizon carries one boundary mode of entropy $\sigma$, giving $S = \sigma N_{\text{modes}} = A / (4\, l_p^2)$. This is the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy in its standard form, and the coefficient does not depend on $\sigma$; the mode count $N_{\text{modes}} = S/\sigma$ equals the entropy only at $\sigma = 1$.

The factor of $1/4$ is therefore fixed, given $\epsilon = 2\sqrt{\sigma}\, l_p$: it is the ratio $\epsilon^2 / (\sigma\, l_p^2) = 4\sigma\, l_p^2 / (\sigma\, l_p^2) = 4$ in the denominator, with the $\epsilon^2$ factor arising from the framework's discreteness scale, the $\sigma$ from the entropy per mode, and the $l_p^2$ in the denominator arising from the Planck length appearing in the definition of $S_{\text{BH}}$. Equivalently, the $1/4$ is the ratio $2\pi / 8\pi$ between two physical quantities: the numerator $2\pi$ is the period of the Euclidean KMS regularity at the horizon (thermal periodicity of QFT on a Killing horizon), and the denominator $8\pi$ is the Jacobson coefficient in the Einstein-equation prefactor $dE = (c^2 \kappa / 8\pi G)\, dA$ (gravity's coupling). The Bekenstein-Hawking $1/4$ is therefore not an arbitrary proportionality constant but the consistency condition between gravitational thermodynamics and quantum thermal physics — the ratio that makes the framework's gap equation in Section 7.3 close on itself.

**Extension to black-hole horizons.** On the single-link lattice branch, the mode count uses horizons that are spherical in the lattice frame or have the same area-averaged link density. Applying the $1/4$ calibration to a black-hole horizon also requires the corresponding stationary, state and frame conditions of Sections 7.3–7.4.

**Classical area test.** Under the classical area theorem's hypotheses, the remnant horizon area is bounded below by the sum of the progenitor areas. The waveform can be used to compare those areas. GW150914 supplied an earlier pre- and post-merger test. [Isi et al.](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.127.011103)

The LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA collaborations observed GW250114 on January 14, 2025 and reported the analysis in September. The fiducial comparison of the inferred Kerr areas supports area increase at $4.4\sigma$. The significance depends on the analysis cut and modes used. [LVK, Fig. 5](https://dcc.ligo.org/public/0201/P2500421/010/GW250114.pdf)

The gravitational-wave comparison addresses the classical areas. The entropy coefficient has a separate thermal test. The coefficient, fixed by the calibration of Section 7.4 as the ratio $2\pi/8\pi$ between Euclidean KMS periodicity and the Jacobson Einstein-equation prefactor, is the ratio of the two normalizations that the calibration matches, conditional on H-slope, H-count, the horizon and frame conditions, local boost structure at the horizon and the symmetric detailed-balance rate ratio ([GR §8.5]), with no direct empirical test at present. The coefficient follows from the Hawking-temperature normalization, so a measured horizon radiation spectrum would fix it. The one contingent channel is the final evaporation burst of a primordial black hole — active all-sky burst searches (Fermi-LAT 2018; HAWC 2020; LHAASO 2025, PRL 135 181005) have so far set rate-density limits only, with next-generation sensitivity projected for CTAO; a detected burst light curve would measure $T(M)$ and hence the coefficient directly. Analogue-gravity experiments probe the kinematic side of the $2\pi/8\pi$ ratio, not the gravitational side. The ongoing LIGO observing runs will accumulate additional events testing the area theorem across a range of progenitor masses and spins.

**Falsification status.** The Bekenstein-Hawking $1/4$ is a consistency relation of the horizon calibration, not a counted Class B prediction (Chapter 4 §4.5) — conditional at the universality-class level, holding under H-slope together with H-count, the horizon and frame conditions, local boost structure at the horizon and the symmetric detailed-balance rate ratio on the gap-equation chain (Section 7.1). Falsification of the coefficient at sufficient confidence to exclude experimental systematics would refute that chain (the connection between the classical horizon temperature in Section 7.3 Step 1 and the Euclidean KMS temperature in Step 4) under those conditions, and with it the framework's structural commitment to thermal self-consistency at the partition boundary. The coefficient currently has no direct empirical test — GW250114 constrains the classical area theorem, not the entropy normalization — the $a_0 = cH/6$ rotation-curve family (Section 7.9) is a conditional measured comparison, with agreement within the quoted systematic uncertainty and the factor $1/6$ imported.

## 7.6 The Page curve and the information paradox

The framework's content in the Bekenstein-Hawking sector extends beyond the area-law coefficient to the *dynamics* of black hole evaporation. The black hole information paradox — Hawking's 1975 observation that semiclassical black hole evaporation appears to destroy information, in tension with quantum mechanics' commitment to unitarity — is one of the canonical hard problems of theoretical physics. The framework does not resolve the paradox. Information is never lost from the substratum bijection $\varphi$ because $\varphi$ is bijective, and bijectivity is a premise of the framework's models (Chapter 1), not a result; how much of that information an observer can recover from the radiation is a separate question, which bijectivity alone does not settle. The Page-curve derivation below bears on it under two conditions, H-scramble and H-decouple, neither of which is established, and H-scramble fails for every bijection acting on an initial configuration with the bipartition aligned to the configuration coordinates. The conditional entropy calculation has its own dynamical assumptions.

**The paradox in its conventional form.** Hawking's calculation of the spectrum of radiation emitted by an evaporating black hole produced a thermal distribution with a temperature $T_H = \hbar \kappa / (2\pi c k_B)$, dependent only on the black hole's mass and angular momentum. The thermal spectrum carries no information beyond the macroscopic horizon parameters. If the radiation is exactly thermal — as Hawking's semiclassical derivation suggests — then the radiation carries no information about the matter that formed the black hole, and the information appears to be lost when the black hole completes its evaporation. This is in tension with the unitarity of quantum mechanics, which requires that any time evolution preserve information.

The Page curve is the unitarity prediction. Don Page argued in 1993 that if black hole evaporation is unitary, the entanglement entropy of the radiation must follow a specific trajectory: rising as radiation accumulates in the early phase, then turning over and decreasing at the *Page time* $t_P$ — defined as the moment when the radiation's entanglement entropy equals the remaining black hole's entropy. If the radiation's entropy equals the horizon entropy it carries off, the Page time comes when half the black hole's initial entropy has been radiated, and earlier if the radiation carries more; the curve's turnover is the empirical signature of unitarity.

The dispute between Hawking's calculation (thermal radiation, information lost) and Page's prediction (Page curve, information preserved) was unresolved for decades. The recent quantum-extremal-surface and entanglement-island programs of Penington, Almheiri-Engelhardt-Marolf-Maxfield, and others have reproduced the Page curve through replica wormhole computations in the gravitational path integral, providing a derivation of the Page curve from semiclassical gravity. The framework offers a conditional alternative through a substantially different mechanism: a derivation from the scrambling condition H-scramble and the decoupling condition H-decouple of [GR Appendix A]. Under those conditions, and taking the energy shell of the black hole and its radiation to be their full product space, the two derivations give a Page curve of the same form, the framework's as an average over the cycles of the dynamics; whether they agree on the Page time depends on the entropy each assigns to the radiation, as set out below.

**The observer's description: nested trace-out.** The framework's substratum is a deterministic bijection $\varphi$ acting on a configuration space $S$, finite under A1. Information is never lost from $\varphi$ in the framework's bijective models — bijectivity is the framework's structural commitment to unitarity in those models. Bijectivity of the whole dynamics leaves the radiation's reduced law open.

The nested calculation considers a cosmological horizon and a black-hole horizon inside it. The configuration space decomposes as
$$S = \mathcal{C}_V \times \mathcal{C}_B \times \mathcal{C}_D,$$
where $V$ is the region between the black hole horizon and the cosmological horizon (the external observer's visible sector), $B$ is the black hole interior (the secondary hidden sector), and $D$ is the trans-cosmological region (the primary hidden sector).

On $V$, both a direct trace over $B,D$ and a successive trace give the same channel for one supplied unitary dilation and a shared product input on $V,B,D$ ([GR Appendix A.2–A.3]). Inferring Hamiltonians from transition data uses the reconstruction hypotheses of [GR §3.3], with the diagonal-unitary, energy-origin and possible antiunitary freedoms. If the common channel family is exactly unitary, the generators differ only by a scalar shift. Agreement of $\hbar$ also requires each horizon to meet the calibration conditions with matched $\epsilon$ and $\sigma$ ([GR Appendix A.4]).

**The Page curve from cycle typicality.** The framework's derivation of the Page curve does not use the gravitational path integral: the cycle structure of the bijection $\varphi$ supplies exact time averages, and the Page value enters through a typicality condition on the substratum orbit. The relevant results are Page's mean entropy (Page 1993) and measure concentration (Popescu, Short & Winter 2006): for a bipartite quantum system in a Haar-random pure state, with subsystem dimensions $d_{\min} \leq d_{\max}$, the entanglement entropy of the smaller subsystem lies close to Page's mean, about $\ln d_{\min} - d_{\min}/(2d_{\max})$ — within half a nat of the maximum $\ln d_{\min}$ when the two dimensions are equal, and closer as the larger one grows — with deviations from that mean exponentially unlikely in the dimension of the smaller subsystem. These results concern Haar-random states of the full product space; on a proper energy shell, a subspace fixed by energy conservation, the mean entropy depends on the shell and in general differs from Page's value, so the derivation below takes the shell to be the full product space of the black hole and its radiation, which places no energy constraint on them; an evaporating black hole whose radiation carries away its energy is the energy-shell case, and that version is open ([GR Appendix A]). Applying them to the substratum requires H-scramble: uniformity over classical microstates is not uniformity over quantum pure states, and every finite bijection acting on a definite configuration, with the bipartition aligned to the configuration coordinates, visits only product states, however uniformly it counts its energy-shell cycles ([GR Appendix A]). H-scramble therefore fails in that setting, which is the one the derivation counts: its initial conditions are configurations, so there the derivation has no instance. At a bipartition aligned with the configuration coordinates, the entanglement entropy a bijection produces never exceeds, at any step, the entropy of the initial state's distribution over configurations ([GR Appendix A]); a Page value therefore requires an initial superposition over at least about $e^{S_{\text{Page}}}$ configurations, with $S_{\text{Page}}$ Page's mean entropy, or a bipartition not aligned with them. Which of these, if either, the substratum supplies is open, and the condition is not established.

Applied to the evaporating black hole, the relevant bipartite structure is the radiation field outside the black hole versus the boundary modes remaining at the shrinking horizon. Let $n_B(t)$ denote the number of boundary modes remaining at the horizon at time $t$, and $n_R(t) = n_B(0) - n_B(t)$ the number of modes radiated. Conservation gives $n_B(t) + n_R(t) = n_B(0)$ — the total number of modes is fixed by the initial black hole entropy, $n_B(0) = S_{\text{BH}}(0)/\sigma$.

Under H-scramble, which carries Popescu-Short-Winter measure concentration from the Haar ensemble to the orbit of the bijection on the full product space of the black hole and its radiation, and under the decoupling condition H-decouple of [GR Appendix A], which treats the black hole and its radiation as a closed system in a pure joint state, the radiation's entanglement entropy, averaged over full cycles of the dynamics at the bipartition of time $t$, is approximately
$$\overline{S}_{\text{rad}}(t) = \min(n_R(t), n_B(t)) \cdot \ln q,$$
where $q$ is the substratum's alphabet size, so that the state space of one mode has dimension $q$, with a correction of at most about half a nat, reached at the turnover and exponentially small in $|n_R(t) - n_B(t)|$ away from it. Whether the entropy along a single trajectory, whose bipartition moves with $t$, follows the same curve is open. The structure has two regimes:

- *Early phase* ($n_R(t) < n_B(t)$): radiation has fewer modes than the remaining black hole; entropy rises as $n_R(t) \ln q$.
- *Late phase* ($n_R(t) > n_B(t)$): radiation has more modes than the remaining black hole; entropy falls as $n_B(t) \ln q$.

The turnover — the Page time $t_P$ — occurs at $n_R(t_P) = n_B(t_P) = n_B(0)/2$, when half the initial boundary modes have been radiated. Under the evaporation law $dM/dt = -\alpha/M^2$, with a mass-independent coefficient $\alpha$ into which the species content and the greybody factors enter ([GR Appendix A]), the mode count falls as $n_B(t) = n_B(0)\,(1 - t/t_{\text{evap}})^{2/3}$, and the Page time is
$$t_P = (1 - 2^{-3/2})\, t_{\text{evap}} \approx 0.646\, t_{\text{evap}},$$
independent of the black hole's mass and of $\alpha$.

The greybody factors enter a second time, through the entropy the radiation carries. The conservation law $n_B(t) + n_R(t) = n_B(0)$ gives the radiation one mode of dimension $q$ for each boundary mode the horizon loses, so the radiation's state-space entropy grows by exactly the amount the horizon's falls. More generally, write $\beta$ for the ratio of the radiation's gain in state-space entropy to the horizon's loss, taken constant along the evaporation; when each mode carries $\sigma = \ln q$, $\beta$ is the radiation's coarse-grained entropy per unit of Bekenstein-Hawking entropy lost. The turnover falls where $\beta\,[S_{\text{BH}}(0) - S_{\text{BH}}(t_P)] = S_{\text{BH}}(t_P)$, and the same evaporation law gives (Page 2013, Eq. (8))
$$\frac{t_P}{t_{\text{evap}}} = 1 - \left(\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}\right)^{3/2},$$
which is $0.646$ at $\beta = 1$ and falls as $\beta$ grows: $0.568$ at $\beta = 4/3$, the value for unfiltered blackbody emission of massless quanta. In the quasi-stationary semiclassical regime — a non-rotating, uncharged black hole emitting free fields into empty space (incoming vacuum), outgoing modes in independent thermal-form states with mean occupation $N = \Gamma\, n_{\text{th}}(\omega/T_H)$ for greybody factors $0 < \Gamma \leq 1$, and $dS_{\text{BH}} = dE/T_H$ — every emitted mode carries more entropy than its energy divided by the Hawking temperature, so $\beta > 1$ in that regime (Page 2005, Eqs. (5)–(10); [GR Appendix A]); its value depends on the species content and on how the greybody factors vary with frequency — solution-specific content, Tier 3 in the sense of Section 7.1. For a large Schwarzschild black hole emitting only photons and gravitons, at constant $\alpha$, Page's emission rates give $\beta \approx 1.485$ and $t_P \approx 0.538\, t_{\text{evap}}$ (Page 2013, Eqs. (2), (8)). The Page time $0.646\, t_{\text{evap}}$ is therefore conditional on $\beta = 1$ as well as on H-scramble and H-decouple: the mode-for-mode conservation law sets $\beta = 1$, the semiclassical regime just defined has $\beta > 1$, and how much state-space entropy the radiation acquires per boundary mode lost is open.

**Comparison with quantum extremal surfaces and islands.** The quantum extremal surface formula and the entanglement-island construction developed by Penington, Almheiri-Engelhardt, and others obtain the Page curve by a different route. In the QES approach, the Page curve emerges through an extremization principle that selects the dominant saddle in a replica-trick computation of the radiation's entanglement entropy. The post-Page-time saddle includes an *island* — a region of bulk spacetime that contributes to the radiation's entanglement entropy despite being topologically disconnected from the radiation region. The replica wormhole geometries connecting the replicas in the gravitational path integral are essential to obtaining the saturating Page curve at late times.

The framework arrives at a cycle-averaged Page curve of the same form — rise, turnover, fall — through a substantially different mechanism. The cycle-typicality argument applies Popescu-Short-Winter measure concentration to the deterministic substratum dynamics on the full product space of the black hole and its radiation, under the scrambling condition H-scramble and the decoupling condition H-decouple of [GR Appendix A] — uniformity over classical microstates is not uniformity over quantum pure states, and neither condition is established — with no replica trick and no gravitational path integral. In the QES computation the turnover comes where the island saddle's generalized entropy, at leading order the black hole's remaining Bekenstein-Hawking entropy, falls below the semiclassical entropy of the radiation. The two Page times coincide when that semiclassical entropy grows one-for-one with the horizon entropy lost, the $\beta = 1$ of the mode counting; in the semiclassical regime defined above $\beta > 1$ holds (Page 2005; for the QES setting, Penington 2019, §2), so agreement between the two derivations on the Page time and the Page entropy is not established. A correspondence between the two constructions is conjectured: the *island* in the QES formulation would correspond to the post-Page-time regime in which the black hole's boundary modes form the smaller subsystem, and the *replica wormhole* geometries to the substratum's cycle structure on the energy shell.

This conjectured correspondence — replica wormholes $\leftrightarrow$ substratum cycles — is suggestive but not yet rigorous. Making it precise would require constructing the gravitational path integral as an effective description of the substratum dynamics in the appropriate limit, a task that has not been completed. What the framework's derivation establishes is conditional: under H-scramble and H-decouple and with the full product space as the energy shell, a cycle-averaged Page curve with its turnover where the radiation's state space overtakes the black hole's follows without the replica machinery. The Page value enters through H-scramble, not through the finite deterministic dynamics, which from an initial configuration produces no entanglement across an aligned bipartition and, across such a bipartition, from any initial state at most the entropy of its distribution over configurations. The open steps include the scrambling condition, the decoupling condition, the value on a proper energy shell, whether the entropy along a single trajectory follows the cycle-averaged curve, and the radiation's entropy per boundary mode lost.

Both approaches require *something* to play the role of typicality — Popescu-Short-Winter measure concentration in the framework's derivation, replica saddles in QES + islands — and both place the turnover where the radiation's entropy reaches the black hole's remaining entropy. If the conjectured correspondence holds, the typicality-providing ingredient need not be gravitational at all, and the cycle-typicality derivation would be a microscopic realization of the entanglement-island construction; on the substratum side the bijection alone does not supply that ingredient, since the entanglement it can produce across an aligned bipartition is bounded by the entropy of the initial distribution over configurations.

**The generalized second law.** A separate but related statement is the generalized second law: the sum $S_{\text{matter}} + S_{\text{BH}} + S_{\text{dS}}$ is non-decreasing in time. The matter entropy $S_{\text{matter}}$ captures the visible-sector matter's information content; the black hole entropy $S_{\text{BH}}$ is the horizon area divided by $4\, l_p^2$; the de Sitter entropy $S_{\text{dS}}$ is the cosmological horizon's contribution.

The nested dynamics gives a bound relative to a product state. Bijectivity makes the evolution of the whole configuration space unitary, so the joint von Neumann entropy of the visible sector $V$, the black hole interior $B$ and the trans-cosmological region $D$ is constant. If the state at $t = 0$ is a product over the three sectors, subadditivity then gives
$$S(\rho_V(t)) + S(\rho_B(t)) + S(\rho_D(t)) \geq S(\rho_V(0)) + S(\rho_B(0)) + S(\rho_D(0))$$
at each time, including when the degrees of freedom are regrouped into three sectors as the horizon moves. The inequality concerns the sectors' von Neumann entropies. A generalized second law requires both an identification of horizon and sector entropies and monotonicity between times. This inequality bounds the sum relative to one product state in both time directions. It allows the sum to rise and then fall. The boundary-mode count does not supply the entropy identification: $S_{\text{BH}} = n_B\,\sigma$ counts the horizon's modes, while in the Page-curve derivation above, where the black hole and its radiation share a pure state, the black hole's reduced entropy equals the radiation's and is at most $n_R \ln q$ — near zero early in the evaporation, when $S_{\text{BH}}$ is largest. Relative entropy is contractive under a CPTP map. Separately, the von Neumann entropy of the output is at least that of the input for every input only when the channel is unital, and then it constrains $S_{\text{matter}}$ alone, not the horizon terms. A derivation of the generalized second law from the substratum requires the area–entropy relation and monotonicity of the combined sum. Conservation of information at the substratum level rests, in the framework's models, on bijectivity alone and does not wait on the generalized second law; how much of that information the observer can recover from the radiation is the separate question the Page-curve derivation addresses, under its own conditions.

**Empirical status and falsification.** The framework's account of the information paradox has no direct empirical signature accessible at current experimental precision — black hole evaporation timescales for astrophysical black holes vastly exceed the age of the universe, and the Page time for a stellar-mass black hole would not occur within any conceivable observational program. The bijective models preserve information in the whole system. The Page-time formula concerns the conditional radiation calculation.

Bijective dynamics is compatible with radiation that carries less information than the matter that formed it, the remainder being held in sectors the observer does not read, and the nested trace-out identity holds with the common dilation and state above; a measurement of the radiation alone therefore does not test the conservation claim, which rests on bijectivity as a premise of the framework's models (Chapter 1). The QES + islands computations, which obtain a unitary Page curve from semiclassical gravity by an independent route, support unitary evaporation without depending on the framework. The framework's content is therefore consistent with the current theoretical and (limited) empirical picture. Its quantitative target is the Page-time relation $t_P/t_{\text{evap}} = 1 - [\beta/(1+\beta)]^{3/2}$, which becomes a sharp prediction once the radiation's entropy per boundary mode lost is derived; at the mode-counting value $\beta = 1$ it gives $0.646$.

## 7.7 Dissolution of the cosmological constant problem

The cosmological constant problem is conventionally described as the worst quantitative discrepancy in physics. Quantum field theory predicts that the vacuum energy density of the universe should be of order $\rho_{\text{QFT}} \sim M_{\text{Pl}}^4 \sim 10^{113}\,\text{J/m}^3$, obtained by summing zero-point energies $\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega$ over all field modes up to the Planck cutoff. The observed vacuum energy density is $\rho_{\text{obs}} \sim H^2/G \sim 10^{-9}\,\text{J/m}^3$, with the ratio $\rho_{\text{QFT}} / \rho_{\text{obs}} \sim 10^{122}$. Standard accounts treat this as a fine-tuning problem requiring an unknown cancellation mechanism to suppress the bare vacuum energy by 122 decimal places, re-imposed at each order of perturbation theory.

The zero-point sum and the supplied cosmological density enter different descriptions. A dissolution of the gravitational discrepancy requires a covariant coupling that decouples a common zero-point offset. That step belongs to G3. [GR §6.2]

**The two levels of description.** The framework's structural commitments produce two distinct descriptions of the universe's energy content, operating at logically different levels.

*The supplied classical background* has critical density $\rho_{\rm crit}=3H^2c^2/(8\pi G)$ in the flat Friedmann model. The observed vacuum density is the supplied fraction $\Omega_\Lambda\rho_{\rm crit}$. Its native source remains open.

*The emergent quantum field theory* assigns zero-point energy to its modes. On the stated lattice branch the cutoff $M_{\rm Pl}/\sqrt{6\sigma}$ gives the order of magnitude $\rho_{\rm QFT}\sim M_{\rm Pl}^4$ when $\sigma$ is of order one. A uniform shift in the emergent generator leaves the ordinary transition probabilities invariant. Relative differences, such as those measured in Casimir forces, compare physical configurations. **What the ordering does not yet fix.** The supplied metric at the partition boundary leaves open the gravitational field of a laboratory mass that the observer's description places in a spatial superposition. **The structural origin of the $10^{122}$ discrepancy.** The ratio $\rho_{\text{QFT}} / \rho_{\text{classical}} \sim M_{\text{Pl}}^4 / (H^2 / G) \sim S_{\text{dS}} = A / (4\, l_p^2)$ is a dimensional identity for any theory with a Planck cutoff and a Hubble-scale density, holding up to a convention-dependent factor. ## 7.9 The dark sector and the MOND acceleration scale

The proposed dark-matter account uses entropy displacement at the cosmological boundary. Direct searches constrain the masses and interactions they test. Detection of a particle species accounting for the structured dark sector would contradict a boundary-only source. [GR §7.2]

**Boundary energy and its Hubble-volume mean.** The boundary count and classical temperature give a normalization. The interpretation as a gravitating source requires the native coupling.

The horizon carries $A_{\text{horizon}} / \epsilon^2$ modes and the entropy $S_{\text{dS}} = \sigma A_{\text{horizon}} / \epsilon^2$ by the boundary-mode counting of Section 7.5.
